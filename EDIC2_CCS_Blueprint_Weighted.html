How to use this resource

This bank contains 200 EDIC Part II–style stations:

  • 50 CCS (20 long, 30 short)
  • 150 CBS (50 imaging, 50 curves/waveforms, 50 biochemistry/data)

All stations are consultant-level, aligned with the EDIC Guidelines 2023–2026 and the CoBaTrICE syllabus.

Suggested approach

CCS stations

  • Long cases: allow yourself 10 minutes to read the opening vignette, then 25 minutes to answer out loud in structured bullet points, as in the real exam.
  • Short cases: 5 minutes reading, 12–13 minutes answering.
  • Answer out loud first, then write your bullet points. If you cannot say it in structured order in 60 seconds, you do not truly have it.
  • Before revealing the model answer, set your confidence: Confident / Unsure / Guessing. Do not adjust it afterwards.
  • Then reveal the model answer and mark yourself as an examiner would. Partial credit is allowed; benefit of the doubt is not.

CBS stations

  • Treat each as a 12-minute station with 8–12 expected answers.
  • Go through the images/curves/data in order, speaking your answers out loud, then write them down.
  • Set confidence before revealing, then mark yourself strictly.

Tracking your progress

  • For each station, record: confidence, marks awarded, and total marks.
  • Periodically review your performance by domain (respiratory, cardiovascular, neuro, etc.) to identify weak areas.
  • Re-sit selected stations after 2–4 weeks without re-reading the model answers first; the delta is the only measure that matters.

Blueprint coverage

  • The distribution of stations follows the EDIC Part II blueprint derived from CoBaTrICE:
    • Heavy emphasis on respiratory, cardiovascular, neurology, and infection.
    • Substantial coverage of renal/metabolic, GI/hepatic, haematology/oncology, endocrine/metabolic-toxic, obstetric, trauma, and end-of-life/ethics.
  • CBS stations emphasise data interpretation (imaging, curves, biochemistry) across all systems.

Use this bank as a calibration and gap-diagnostic tool, not just a question list. The goal is to expose guideline drift, strengthen structured verbal answers, and build exam-ready fluency.

Sample Stations

CCS_L01 — Severe ARDS with Consideration of ECMO

Long case Respiratory CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4·6·7·11·12 40 marks · 10 min read / 25 min answer

Opening vignette

A 34-year-old previously fit man is admitted to ICU with severe community-acquired pneumonia. He is now on day 2 of invasive mechanical ventilation. Current settings: volume-control, tidal volume 6 mL/kg predicted body weight, PEEP 14 cmH2O, FiO2 1.0. Plateau pressure 32 cmH2O, driving pressure 18 cmH2O. Arterial blood gas: pH 7.29, PaCO2 6.8 kPa (51 mmHg), PaO2 9.0 kPa (68 mmHg) → PaO2/FiO2 ratio ≈ 68 mmHg. Chest X-ray shows bilateral diffuse airspace opacification. He is on noradrenaline 0.15 micrograms/kg/min, lactate 2.1 mmol/L, and a bedside echo shows preserved LV and RV function with no significant valvular disease.

Structured questions

  1. Part A — Assessment and diagnosis (10 min). How do you define and classify this patient's condition? What is your differential diagnosis for bilateral pulmonary infiltrates with severe hypoxaemia, and how would you exclude the main alternative diagnosis at the bedside?
  2. Part B — Immediate ventilatory management (10 min). Outline your lung-protective ventilation strategy and the evidence base for it. What adjuncts would you consider next, and on what evidence?
  3. Part C — Deterioration (5 min). Despite 16 hours of prone positioning, his PaO2/FiO2 ratio remains 65 mmHg, PaCO2 rises to 9.3 kPa (70 mmHg) and pH falls to 7.15 on unchanged lung-protective settings. What are your options now, and how would you decide whether to refer for veno-venous ECMO?
  4. Part D — Communication and ethics. The regional ECMO centre declines transfer, citing 9 days of high-pressure ventilation and evolving multi-organ dysfunction. How do you communicate this to the family, and what does your subsequent management plan look like?
Reveal model answer

Part A — Assessment and diagnosis

  • Apply the Berlin Definition of ARDS: onset within 1 week of a known clinical insult or new/worsening respiratory symptoms; bilateral opacities on chest imaging not fully explained by effusion, lobar/lung collapse, or nodules; respiratory failure not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload (objective assessment, e.g. echocardiography, required if no risk factor present); oxygenation deficit graded on ≥5 cmH2O PEEP/CPAP: mild PaO2/FiO2 201–300 mmHg, moderate 101–200 mmHg, severe ≤100 mmHg.
  • This patient meets criteria for severe ARDS (P/F ≈ 68 mmHg) secondary to severe CAP.
  • Differentials for bilateral infiltrates with severe hypoxaemia: cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, diffuse alveolar haemorrhage, acute eosinophilic pneumonia, acute interstitial pneumonia, aspiration, transfusion- related lung injury (if relevant history), miliary TB/fungal disease in the right epidemiological context.
  • At the bedside: focused echocardiography (LV/RV function, filling, valves — already reassuring here), clinical trajectory/sepsis source, consider NT-proBNP if diagnostic uncertainty persists, review fluid balance and CVP/extravascular lung water if available; bronchoscopy with BAL if atypical course or immunocompromise.

Part B — Immediate ventilatory management

  • Lung-protective ventilation: tidal volume 4–8 mL/kg predicted body weight (target 6 mL/kg), plateau pressure <30 cmH2O, driving pressure as low as achievable (ideally <15 cmH2O — this patient's driving pressure of 18 is already a red flag), permissive hypercapnia accepted if pH tolerated (ARDSNet/ARMA trial, NEJM 2000).
  • PEEP: titrate using a higher-PEEP strategy for moderate–severe ARDS (e.g. ARDSNet high-PEEP/FiO2 table, or decremental PEEP trial guided by compliance/driving pressure); avoid PEEP that worsens driving pressure or causes haemodynamic compromise.
  • Prone positioning for ≥16 hours/day is indicated now (P/F <150 mmHg) — the PROSEVA trial (Guérin et al., NEJM 2013) showed a 28-day and 90-day mortality benefit with early, prolonged proning in severe ARDS. This should be instituted promptly, not held in reserve as a rescue therapy.
  • Neuromuscular blockade: consider a short period of neuromuscular blockade if there is patient–ventilator dyssynchrony or breath-stacking despite optimised sedation, rather than as a routine 48-hour infusion for every patient with P/F <150 (see drift trap below).
  • Conservative fluid management once shock resolved (FACTT trial principles); avoid unnecessary positive fluid balance.
  • Ensure adequate sedation/analgesia, lung-protective settings maintained even during transport or procedures, and daily reassessment of prone/supine cycling, weaning readiness and driving pressure.

Part C — Deterioration and ECMO referral

  • First re-confirm optimisation is genuine: adequate proning duration/technique, PEEP/recruitment appropriately titrated, sedation/neuromuscular blockade adequate, rule out a new complication (pneumothorax, mucus plugging, worsening infection, new cardiac dysfunction).
  • Consider a trial of inhaled pulmonary vasodilator (nitric oxide or prostacyclin) as a bridge — improves oxygenation transiently in a subset, no proven mortality benefit, useful while arranging transfer or managing severe hypoxaemia/RV strain.
  • This patient now has refractory hypoxaemia and hypercapnic acidosis despite optimised lung-protective ventilation and proning — a standard indication to refer for veno-venous ECMO, per criteria informed by the EOLIA trial (Combes et al., NEJM 2018): P/F <50 mmHg for >3 h, P/F <80 mmHg for >6 h, or pH <7.25 with PaCO2 ≥60 mmHg for >6 h despite optimised care.
  • Be able to discuss EOLIA honestly: the trial's primary 60-day mortality outcome was not statistically significant (relative risk 0.76, 95% CI 0.55–1.04), but it was stopped early for futility/safety boundary crossing, and 28% of the control arm crossed over to ECMO as rescue — this materially confounds interpretation. Post-hoc Bayesian re-analysis and individual-patient-data meta-analyses (including CESAR) suggest a likely mortality benefit in patients meeting these severity criteria.
  • Refer early rather than late — outcomes worsen with each additional day of high-pressure/high-FiO2 ventilation before cannulation; discuss with the regional ECMO centre before the patient is unsalvageable, and arrange safe mobile ECMO retrieval if accepted.
  • Know the relative contraindications (ELSO guidance): prolonged high-pressure ventilation (typically >7–10 days), significant pre-existing CNS injury with poor prognosis, uncontrolled multi-organ failure, severe irreversible comorbidity, and severe immunosuppression — these are relative and considered in the context of the whole patient, not absolute cut-offs.

Part D — Communication and ongoing management if ECMO declined

  • Communicate honestly and compassionately: explain what has been tried, why ECMO was considered, and why the receiving centre felt it was unlikely to change the outcome given prolonged high-pressure ventilation and evolving organ failure — avoid false reassurance or minimising the severity.
  • Use a structured approach (e.g. SPIKES or equivalent), ensure a senior clinician leads the discussion, check understanding, allow silence and questions, and document the conversation.
  • Re-focus the management plan on optimising every reversible element of conventional therapy: continued lung-protective ventilation, proning, neuromuscular blockade if dyssynchronous, treatment of the underlying infection, nutrition, and daily review of organ support.
  • Proactively revisit goals of care with the family as the multidisciplinary team's assessment evolves; if the trajectory continues to worsen despite maximal conventional therapy, initiate early, structured discussions about ceilings of care and, if appropriate, a shift to comfort-focused care — this is not a failure of critical care but part of it.
  • Ensure consistency of messaging across the team, offer psychological/spiritual support, and document a clear escalation/de-escalation plan for the out-of-hours team.
Marking guide

Part A (10 marks): full Berlin definition (4), correct severity classification (2), at least 3 relevant differentials (2), a bedside method to exclude cardiogenic oedema (2).

Part B (10 marks): correct tidal volume/plateau/driving pressure targets (3), PEEP strategy (2), timely proning with correct evidence (3), appropriately nuanced neuromuscular blockade answer (2 — see drift trap).

Part C (10 marks): systematic exclusion of reversible causes before escalating (2), mention of inhaled pulmonary vasodilator as bridge (1), correct EOLIA-based referral criteria (4), balanced critique of EOLIA evidence (2), timeliness of referral (1).

Part D (10 marks): structured breaking-bad-news approach (3), avoidance of false reassurance (2), concrete ongoing management plan (3), proactive goals-of-care planning (2).

Guideline-drift trap: Older recall material teaches "routine 48-hour cisatracurium infusion for all patients with P/F <150" based on the ACURASYS trial (Papazian et al., NEJM 2010). The subsequent ROSE trial (National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute PETAL Network, NEJM 2019) compared early continuous neuromuscular blockade with deep sedation against a strategy of light sedation with as-needed neuromuscular blockade, and found no difference in 90-day mortality — the trial was stopped for futility. Current best practice (and current ESICM/ATS guidance) is to reserve neuromuscular blockade for patients with ongoing dyssynchrony, breath-stacking, or refractory hypoxaemia despite optimised sedation, rather than applying it routinely and continuously to every patient meeting the ACURASYS oxygenation threshold. Candidates who state ACURASYS as current unqualified practice without acknowledging ROSE should lose marks.

CCS_S01 — Septic Shock: Vasopressor Escalation and Steroid Decisions

Short case Sepsis / Infection CoBaTrICE 1·3·4·11 20 marks · 5 min read / 12–13 min answer

Opening vignette

A 58-year-old woman is admitted with septic shock secondary to a perforated sigmoid diverticulum, now 6 hours post source-control laparotomy. She has received 30 mL/kg of balanced crystalloid and is on noradrenaline 0.6 micrograms/kg/min with a MAP of 60 mmHg. Lactate is 4.8 mmol/L (was 6.2 mmol/L on admission). She is anuric for the last 2 hours despite a normal preoperative creatinine.

Structured questions

  1. Summarise your immediate assessment and resuscitation priorities at this point.
  2. Her noradrenaline requirement continues to climb. What is your approach to second-agent vasopressor therapy, and what is the evidence for your choice?
  3. Would you start corticosteroids? Justify your answer with reference to the trial evidence, including where the evidence is inconsistent.
  4. How would you monitor response and plan de-escalation over the next 24–48 hours?
Reveal model answer

1. Immediate assessment and priorities

  • Confirm source control has been effective (drain output, imaging if concern), send repeat cultures if not already done, review antimicrobial choice/spectrum and de-escalate per culture data when available.
  • Reassess volume status dynamically (passive leg raise, pulse pressure variation if ventilated and appropriate, echo) rather than giving further boluses reflexively — she has already received 30 mL/kg and is oliguric/anuric, which may reflect AKI rather than pure hypovolaemia.
  • Target MAP ≥65 mmHg (individualise upward if chronically hypertensive); trend lactate (already falling, a reassuring sign) rather than chasing a normal value acutely.
  • Assess for a surgical or mechanical complication if trajectory doesn't fit (anastomotic leak, ongoing contamination, abdominal compartment syndrome).

2. Second-agent vasopressor therapy

  • Noradrenaline remains first-line; at doses in the range of 0.25–0.5 micrograms/kg/min with ongoing hypotension, current Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidance suggests adding vasopressin rather than simply escalating noradrenaline further.
  • Evidence base: the VASST trial (Russell et al., NEJM 2008) found no overall mortality difference between vasopressin and noradrenaline, though a hypothesis-generating subgroup with less severe shock appeared to benefit. Vasopressin's practical advantages are a catecholamine- sparing effect and possibly less tachyarrhythmia.
  • If shock remains refractory despite noradrenaline plus vasopressin, adrenaline can be added as a third agent; consider hydrocortisone (see below) and reassess for an unrecognised cause (adrenal insufficiency, cardiac dysfunction, ongoing source, acidaemia impairing catecholamine responsiveness).

3. Corticosteroids

  • Yes — this patient, with an escalating noradrenaline requirement well above 0.25 micrograms/kg/min, meets a reasonable threshold to start IV hydrocortisone 200 mg/day (as a continuous infusion or in divided doses).
  • Evidence is genuinely mixed and candidates should say so: CORTICUS (Sprung et al., NEJM 2008) showed no survival benefit from hydrocortisone regardless of ACTH-stimulation response, but faster shock reversal. ADRENAL (Venkatesh et al., NEJM 2018) — hydrocortisone alone — showed faster resolution of shock but no 90-day mortality benefit. APROCCHSS (Annane et al., NEJM 2018) — hydrocortisone plus fludrocortisone — did show a 90-day all-cause mortality benefit.
  • The discrepancy between ADRENAL (hydrocortisone alone, no mortality signal) and APROCCHSS (hydrocortisone + fludrocortisone, mortality benefit) is unresolved; the Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 gives a weak recommendation for IV corticosteroids in adults with septic shock and an ongoing vasopressor requirement, without mandating fludrocortisone co-administration everywhere. Local protocol should be followed, and this uncertainty should be explicitly acknowledged rather than presented as settled.

4. Monitoring and de-escalation

  • Serial lactate clearance, trend MAP/vasopressor dose, urine output and creatinine (renal recovery vs need for RRT), and clinical markers of perfusion — capillary refill time is a reasonable adjunct (ANDROMEDA-SHOCK, Hernández et al., JAMA 2019: CRT-targeted resuscitation trended toward better outcomes than lactate-targeted resuscitation, though the primary endpoint was not statistically significant in the frequentist analysis).
  • Avoid ongoing large-volume fluid administration once shock is stabilising — a restrictive fluid strategy after initial resuscitation has not shown harm and may reduce fluid overload (CLASSIC trial, Meyhoff et al., NEJM 2022: restrictive vs liberal IV fluids in septic shock, no significant difference in 90-day mortality, supporting a more conservative approach as reasonable).
  • Wean vasopressors as MAP is maintained on falling doses; taper hydrocortisone rather than stopping abruptly once vasopressors are off; reassess antimicrobial duration/de-escalation daily; screen for new organ dysfunction (AKI progression, ileus, ICU-acquired weakness) as she recovers.
Marking guide

Q1 (5 marks): dynamic (not reflexive) fluid assessment (2), source-control/antimicrobial review (1), correct MAP target and lactate-trend reasoning (2).

Q2 (5 marks): correct threshold/rationale for adding vasopressin (3), accurate VASST summary (2).

Q3 (5 marks): correct decision to start hydrocortisone with threshold (2), accurate, balanced summary of CORTICUS/ADRENAL/APROCCHSS (3 — see drift trap).

Q4 (5 marks): appropriate monitoring parameters (2), restrictive fluid strategy post-resuscitation (2), safe weaning plan (1).

Guideline-drift trap: Many recall-based answers still state that fludrocortisone is a standard, unconditional addition to hydrocortisone in septic shock. In fact, ADRENAL (hydrocortisone alone) showed no mortality benefit while APROCCHSS (hydrocortisone plus fludrocortisone) did — the two major contemporary trials used different regimens and reached different mortality conclusions. Examiners should expect candidates to acknowledge this inconsistency explicitly rather than quoting a single trial as if it were the whole picture.

CCS_L02 — Hypercapnic COPD Exacerbation: NIV, Ventilation Strategy and Tracheostomy Decision-Making

Long case Respiratory CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4·5·7·11 40 marks · 10 min read / 25 min answer

Opening vignette

A 68-year-old woman with known COPD (FEV1 38% predicted, on home oxygen) presents with an infective exacerbation. Arterial blood gas on controlled 28% oxygen: pH 7.28, PaCO2 6.8 kPa (51 mmHg), PaO2 7.9 kPa (59 mmHg), bicarbonate 28 mmol/L. She is started on non-invasive ventilation (NIV) in the emergency department alongside nebulised bronchodilators, steroids and antibiotics.

Structured questions

  1. Part A (10 min). What are your indications for starting NIV here, your initial settings, and how will you monitor for early success or failure?
  2. Part B (10 min). After 2 hours, she is more drowsy; repeat gas shows pH 7.19, PaCO2 8.9 kPa (67 mmHg). What do you do now, and if intubated, how does your ventilation strategy differ from a patient without airflow obstruction?
  3. Part C (5 min). By day 10 she remains ventilator-dependent with repeated failed spontaneous breathing trials. Outline your weaning approach and your plan for extubation.
  4. Part D. Weaning continues to fail. How do you approach the tracheostomy decision, and what wider issues does this raise for this patient?
Reveal model answer

Part A — NIV initiation and monitoring

  • Indications: acute hypercapnic respiratory failure with respiratory acidosis (pH <7.35, PaCO2 >6 kPa) persisting despite optimal medical therapy — met here (pH 7.28).
  • Initial settings: pressure-support/bilevel mode, start EPAP ~4–5 cmH2O and IPAP ~10–12 cmH2O, titrate IPAP upward in increments to reduce work of breathing and PaCO2, target SpO2 88–92% (controlled oxygen, avoid hyperoxia-driven hypercapnia).
  • Monitor: repeat ABG at 1 hour is the key predictor of success/failure (BTS/RCP audit-based practice); also track respiratory rate, consciousness level, accessory muscle use, and patient tolerance/synchrony continuously.
  • Early failure predictors: worsening pH/PaCO2 or unchanged acidosis at 1 hour, falling GCS, haemodynamic instability, inability to tolerate the interface — these should prompt early escalation planning (ceiling-of-care discussion and/or intubation readiness) rather than persisting with NIV alone.

Part B — NIV failure and intubation strategy

  • This is NIV failure (worsening acidosis and consciousness despite adequate trial) — proceed to intubation and invasive ventilation unless a pre-agreed ceiling of care precludes this; do not persist with NIV in a deteriorating, drowsy patient.
  • Ventilation strategy differs from a non-obstructed patient principally around avoiding dynamic hyperinflation: use a lower respiratory rate, prolong expiratory time (adjust I:E ratio, e.g. 1:3–1:4), accept a higher inspiratory flow to shorten inspiratory time, and permit permissive hypercapnia (target pH >7.20–7.25 rather than normocapnia).
  • Set extrinsic PEEP cautiously to roughly 70–85% of measured intrinsic PEEP once the patient is triggering spontaneously, to offset trigger workload — but not routinely in a fully controlled, paralysed patient (same principle as CBS_CRV_01).
  • Continue aggressive bronchodilation, treat the underlying trigger (infection), and monitor plateau pressure and measured auto-PEEP rather than peak pressure alone.

Part C — Weaning approach

  • Daily assessment of weaning readiness: resolving precipitant, adequate oxygenation (P/F >150–200 on modest support), haemodynamic stability, adequate cough/secretion clearance, awake and cooperative.
  • Perform a structured spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) once readiness criteria are met (e.g. low-level pressure support or T-piece for 30–120 minutes), assessing rapid shallow breathing index, work of breathing, gas exchange and haemodynamics.
  • Consider a diaphragm-protective approach — avoid both over-assistance (disuse atrophy) and under-assistance (fatigue) during the weaning phase; treat reversible contributors to weaning failure (electrolyte derangement, fluid overload, undernutrition, ICU-acquired weakness, undertreated bronchospasm).
  • If extubation is attempted, plan for a high-risk extubation given underlying COPD: consider planned post-extubation NIV to prevent reintubation (see evidence below), have a clear reintubation trigger plan.

Part D — Tracheostomy decision and wider issues

  • Tracheostomy is considered for anticipated prolonged ventilator dependence, to improve comfort, reduce sedation requirement, facilitate weaning trials and rehabilitation, and improve secretion clearance — not as a default step after a fixed number of failed extubations.
  • Timing: the TracMan trial (Young et al., JAMA 2013) found no significant difference in 30-day mortality, ICU length of stay, or 2-year mortality between early (within 4 days) and late (after 10 days) tracheostomy — timing should be individualised rather than protocolised to "early is always better."
  • Given her severe underlying COPD and now prolonged, difficult-to-wean respiratory failure, this is also the point to proactively revisit goals of care with the patient (if able to participate) and family: realistic long-term prognosis, likelihood of ventilator liberation, quality of life with possible long-term tracheostomy/home ventilation, and her own previously expressed wishes.
  • Involve respiratory medicine, physiotherapy, and palliative care early in parallel with ongoing active treatment — these are not mutually exclusive, and early integration improves decision quality and family experience.
Marking guide

Part A (10): correct indications/thresholds (3), sensible initial settings (2), correct emphasis on 1-hour gas as key predictor (3), appropriate failure criteria (2).

Part B (10): correct recognition of NIV failure requiring intubation (2), permissive hypercapnia and prolonged expiratory time strategy (4), correct/cautious extrinsic PEEP reasoning (2), ongoing bronchodilation/cause treatment (2).

Part C (10): structured readiness assessment (3), correct SBT approach (3), identification of reversible weaning-failure contributors (2), sensible extubation risk plan including post-extubation NIV (2).

Part D (10): correct, evidence-based (non-dogmatic) timing discussion (4), holistic indication for tracheostomy beyond "failed extubation" (2), proactive goals-of-care discussion (4).

Guideline-drift trap: Older teaching often states "early tracheostomy improves outcomes" as settled fact. The TracMan trial (2013) — the largest RCT on this question — found no significant survival or length-of-stay benefit for early over late tracheostomy. Candidates who assert early tracheostomy as evidence-based best practice without qualification should lose marks.

CCS_L03 — Cardiogenic Shock Complicating Acute MI: Inotropes and Mechanical Circulatory Support

Long case Cardiovascular CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4·5·11·12 40 marks · 10 min read / 25 min answer

Opening vignette

A 62-year-old man is admitted to ICU three hours after primary PCI to the LAD for an anterior STEMI. He is now hypotensive (BP 78/50 mmHg), cool and mottled peripherally, with a rising lactate (5.2 mmol/L) and falling urine output. He is on dobutamine and noradrenaline. Bedside echocardiography shows a severely impaired LV (EF ~20%) with no significant mechanical complication (no VSD, no significant MR, no tamponade) and a normally functioning RV.

Structured questions

  1. Part A (7 min). How do you define and stage this patient's shock state, and what is your systematic bedside assessment?
  2. Part B (8 min). Critique his current pharmacological support and justify your preferred inotrope/vasopressor strategy.
  3. Part C (7 min). Despite optimised pharmacological therapy he remains in refractory shock. Discuss your approach to mechanical circulatory support, including the current evidence for each option.
  4. Part D (3 min). How do you structure the ongoing multidisciplinary and family communication around this decision?
Reveal model answer

Part A — Definition, staging, assessment

  • Cardiogenic shock: primary cardiac dysfunction causing tissue hypoperfusion, typically with hypotension, elevated filling pressures, and end-organ hypoperfusion (oliguria, lactate, altered mentation) not explained by hypovolaemia alone.
  • Stage using the SCAI shock classification (A "at risk" → E "extremis"); this patient — hypotensive, hypoperfused, on two agents but not yet deteriorating to refractory arrest/multi-organ collapse — is broadly SCAI stage C ("classic" cardiogenic shock), trending toward D if worsening despite escalating support.
  • Bedside assessment: focused echo (chamber size/function, valve pathology, pericardial effusion, RV function), invasive arterial line, consider central venous/cardiac output monitoring, 12-lead ECG and repeat troponin to exclude ongoing ischaemia/mechanical complication, review the PCI result for stent patency concerns.

Part B — Pharmacological strategy

  • Norepinephrine remains the preferred first-line vasopressor in cardiogenic shock to maintain coronary/organ perfusion pressure; dobutamine is a reasonable inotrope choice for augmenting cardiac output in a vasopressor-supported patient with preserved-enough blood pressure.
  • Avoid epinephrine as first-line where alternatives are adequate — it is associated with higher lactate, more tachyarrhythmia, and signals of worse metabolic/renal outcomes compared with norepinephrine–dobutamine combinations in comparative studies of cardiogenic shock.
  • Reassess volume status before escalating vasoactive drugs further — under- or over-filling both worsen cardiac output in severe LV dysfunction; titrate to a clear haemodynamic target (MAP ≥65 mmHg, improving lactate/urine output) rather than an arbitrary dose ceiling.

Part C — Mechanical circulatory support

  • Intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP): the IABP-SHOCK II trial (Thiele et al., NEJM 2012, with consistent long-term follow-up) showed no mortality benefit from routine IABP in AMI-cardiogenic shock — it is no longer recommended as routine therapy, though may still have a role for specific mechanical complications or as an adjunct in select cases.
  • Percutaneous microaxial LV support (e.g. Impella CP): the DanGer Shock trial (Møller et al., NEJM 2024) showed a significant reduction in 180-day mortality with early Impella CP versus standard care in AMI-cardiogenic shock, but at the cost of significantly higher rates of adverse events — major bleeding, limb ischaemia, and need for renal replacement therapy. This represents genuinely new, practice-relevant evidence.
  • VA-ECMO: the ECLS-SHOCK trial (Thiele et al., NEJM 2023) — VA-ECMO plus usual care versus usual care alone in infarct-related cardiogenic shock with planned revascularisation — found no significant difference in 30-day all-cause mortality, with more bleeding and peripheral vascular complications in the ECMO arm. VA-ECMO retains a role for biventricular failure, refractory arrest, or as a bridge to decision/transplant/durable device, but is not a proven mortality-reducing default for AMI-cardiogenic shock.
  • Device choice should be individualised (LV-only vs biventricular failure, vascular access, institutional expertise, exit strategy) and discussed with a shock/MCS multidisciplinary team rather than applied reflexively.

Part D — Communication

  • Early, honest discussion with the family about the severity of shock, the specific evidence and trade-offs (mortality benefit vs complication risk) behind any proposed device, and realistic best/worst-case trajectories.
  • Involve cardiology, cardiac surgery/MCS team, and critical care jointly; document a clear escalation ceiling and a plan for de-escalation/withdrawal if the chosen support fails to reverse shock or if complications (e.g. limb ischaemia, major bleeding, irreversible multi-organ failure) supervene.

Part C (15): accurate IABP-SHOCK II summary and correct conclusion (4), accurate DanGer Shock summary including the complication trade-off (5), accurate ECLS-SHOCK summary including the neutral mortality result (4), individualised device selection reasoning (2).

Part D (5): structured MDT and family communication with honest trade-off discussion (5).

Guideline-drift trap: Many candidates default to "escalate mechanical support = better outcome." The current trial evidence is more nuanced and genuinely contradicts this: IABP shows no benefit, DanGer Shock shows a mortality benefit for Impella CP specifically but with materially higher complication rates, and ECLS-SHOCK shows VA-ECMO does not reduce mortality despite intuitive physiological appeal. Device-specific, trial-based reasoning is required rather than a general "more support is better" heuristic.

CCS_L04 — Severe Traumatic Brain Injury: ICP/CPP Management and Prognostication

Long case Neurology / Neurocritical Care CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4·5·7·8·11·12 40 marks · 10 min read / 25 min answer

Opening vignette

A 24-year-old man sustains a severe traumatic brain injury in a high-speed road traffic collision (GCS 3 at scene, now intubated). CT head shows bifrontal contusions and diffuse axonal injury with 4 mm midline shift and effaced basal cisterns. An intraparenchymal ICP monitor is inserted; opening pressure is 28 mmHg.

Structured questions

  1. Part A (8 min). Outline your first-tier goals and therapies for managing raised ICP in the first hours.
  2. Part B (9 min). ICP remains >25 mmHg despite first-tier measures. Discuss your second-tier options and the evidence behind each.
  3. Part C (5 min). What role, if any, does multimodal neuromonitoring play beyond ICP and CPP targets?
  4. Part D (3 min). How do you approach prognostication and communication with the family over the following days?
Reveal model answer

Part A — First-tier management

  • Targets per the Brain Trauma Foundation 4th edition guidelines and the Seattle International Severe TBI Consensus Conference (SIBICC) algorithm: ICP <22 mmHg, CPP typically 60–70 mmHg (individualised, avoiding aggressive CPP augmentation beyond need).
  • First-tier measures: 30° head-up positioning with neutral neck alignment, adequate sedation and analgesia, normocapnia (avoid unintentional hyper- or hypocapnia), normothermia, avoidance of hyponatraemia/hypotonic fluids, treatment of seizures, and CSF drainage via EVD if in place.
  • Ensure adequate oxygenation and MAP to maintain CPP, and correct coagulopathy given contusional injury with expansion risk.

Part B — Second-tier options and evidence

  • Hyperosmolar therapy: mannitol or hypertonic saline, both reasonable — hypertonic saline may be preferred in hypovolaemic/hypotensive patients as it does not cause an osmotic diuresis; evidence does not clearly favour one over the other for outcome.
  • Barbiturate coma: can reduce refractory ICP but with weak outcome evidence and significant risk of hypotension/immunosuppression — reserved for truly refractory cases with haemodynamic support available.
  • Decompressive craniectomy: DECRA (Cooper et al., NEJM 2011) — early bifrontal craniectomy for diffuse injury reduced ICP but was associated with worse functional outcome. RESCUEicp (Hutchinson et al., NEJM 2016) — craniectomy for refractory ICP reduced mortality but with a higher proportion of survivors in a vegetative state or with severe disability; overall favourable outcome rates were similar between groups. This is not a straightforward "life-saving" intervention and must be framed to families as a genuine trade-off, not a rescue cure.
  • Therapeutic hypothermia: Eurotherm3235 (Andrews et al., NEJM 2015) — hypothermia as a second-tier ICP therapy was associated with worse outcomes than standard care and the trial was stopped early for harm. Hypothermia should not be used as a routine second-tier ICP therapy despite the physiological rationale.

Part C — Multimodal monitoring

  • Fixed ICP/CPP thresholds may not reflect individual autoregulatory status; adjuncts include brain tissue oxygen tension (PbtO2) monitoring to detect regional ischaemia despite "normal" ICP, cerebral microdialysis (lactate/pyruvate ratio, glucose) for metabolic crisis detection, and autoregulation indices (e.g. pressure reactivity index, PRx) to individualise optimal CPP rather than applying a fixed population target.
  • Evidence for outcome benefit from PbtO2-guided therapy is still maturing (e.g. BOOST-3) — candidates should present this as a promising adjunct for individualising care rather than a guideline-mandated standard.

Part D — Prognostication and communication

  • Avoid premature prognostication or withdrawal decisions within the first 72 hours — early clinical findings and even early imaging can both underestimate and overestimate eventual recovery potential, particularly in younger patients.
  • Use validated prediction models (IMPACT, CRASH) as adjuncts to, not replacements for, clinical judgement and serial reassessment; communicate uncertainty honestly with the family.
  • Maintain regular, structured, multidisciplinary family updates; if the trajectory suggests catastrophic, non-survivable injury, initiate brain death testing per protocol when clinically appropriate and raise organ donation according to local pathways and the family's own wishes.

Eurotherm3235 specifically tested hypothermia as second-tier ICP therapy and found it caused worse functional outcomes than standard care, stopping early for harm — candidates proposing routine hypothermia for refractory ICP without acknowledging this should lose marks. Similarly, decompressive craniectomy should never be presented as an unambiguous "good" intervention — RESCUEicp's mortality benefit came with more survivors in severe disability/vegetative states, a trade-off central to informed family discussion.

CCS_L05 — Necrotising Soft Tissue Infection with Septic Shock

Long case Sepsis / Infection CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4·5·6·11·12 40 marks · 10 min read / 25 min answer

Opening vignette

A 45-year-old man with type 2 diabetes presents with rapidly progressive pain and swelling of the left leg, out of proportion to the visible findings, with scattered bullae. He is tachycardic (130/min), hypotensive (85/50 mmHg), lactate 6.5 mmol/L, white cell count 2.1 ×109/L (leucopenia), CRP 380 mg/L.

Structured questions

  1. Part A (8 min). What is your leading diagnosis, how confident are you, and what is the role (and limitations) of clinical scoring systems and imaging here?
  2. Part B (7 min). Outline your immediate resuscitation and empirical antimicrobial strategy.
  3. Part C (7 min). Discuss source control: timing, extent, and the evidence for adjunctive therapies that are sometimes proposed.
  4. Part D (3 min). What are the key considerations in his ongoing ICU course and communication with him/his family?
Reveal model answer

Part A — Diagnosis and the role of scores/imaging

  • This presentation — pain out of proportion, rapid progression, bullae, systemic toxicity with leucopenia (a marker of overwhelming infection) — is highly suspicious for a necrotising soft tissue infection (NSTI), a surgical emergency.
  • The LRINEC score can support suspicion but has poor sensitivity and was derived primarily as a rule-in, not a rule-out, tool — a low score must never be used to defer surgical exploration when clinical suspicion is high.
  • CT/MRI may show fascial oedema/gas but should not delay surgery in a clinically obvious case; definitive diagnosis is surgical — direct visualisation of grey, non-bleeding, easily dissecting ("finger test" positive) fascial planes.

Part B — Resuscitation and antimicrobials

  • Standard sepsis bundle: cultures (blood, and intraoperative tissue when source control occurs), balanced crystalloid resuscitation guided dynamically, vasopressors (norepinephrine) if MAP not achieved with fluids, early broad-spectrum antibiotics.
  • Empirical coverage for polymicrobial/toxin-producing organisms: broad-spectrum beta-lactam (piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem) plus clindamycin (for its antitoxin, protein-synthesis-inhibiting effect against toxin-producing streptococci/staphylococci), plus consideration of MRSA cover (vancomycin/linezolid) pending local epidemiology and risk factors.

Part C — Source control and adjuncts

  • Urgent surgical debridement is the single most important intervention — every hour of delay is associated with increased mortality; do not wait for imaging or haemodynamic "optimisation" to proceed to theatre once diagnosis is clinically established.
  • Radical debridement of all non-viable tissue, with planned second-look surgery at 24–48 hours (and repeated as needed) is standard; amputation may be required for limb-threatening/uncontrolled infection.
  • IV immunoglobulin: proposed as adjunctive therapy to neutralise circulating exotoxin, but the INSTINCT trial (Madsen et al.) found no significant benefit on SOFA score/mortality — its routine use is not supported by robust evidence and should not be presented as standard of care.
  • Hyperbaric oxygen: theoretical rationale, no robust RCT evidence of benefit, and must never delay surgical source control — logistically often impractical in an unstable patient.

Part D — Ongoing ICU course and communication

  • Anticipate a prolonged, catabolic critical illness: multi-organ support, aggressive nutrition (high protein/energy requirements), meticulous wound care between debridements, analgesia (often complex given repeated procedures), and early rehabilitation planning.
  • Address the psychological impact of major tissue loss/potential amputation and disfigurement early — involve psychology, plastics/reconstructive surgery, and rehabilitation medicine.
  • Maintain honest, structured communication with the patient (as he recovers capacity) and family about prognosis, the possibility of further surgery/amputation, and realistic functional expectations.
Marking guide

Part A (10): correct diagnosis with justification (4), correct critique of LRINEC (3), correct statement that imaging must not delay surgery (3).

Part B (10): appropriate resuscitation bundle (3), correct broad-spectrum plus clindamycin rationale (5), reasonable MRSA cover discussion (2).

Part C (12): emphasis on urgency/time-critical debridement (4), planned second-look surgery (2), correct, evidence-based (sceptical) IVIG discussion (4), correct dismissal of hyperbaric oxygen as a delay risk (2).

Part D (8): holistic ongoing ICU/nutrition/rehab plan (4), psychological and communication considerations (4).

Guideline-drift trap: LRINEC is frequently misused in recall material as a reliable rule-out test ("low score, can observe"). Its original validation and subsequent evaluations show limited sensitivity — a low score does not exclude NSTI, and diagnosis/decision to operate must rest on clinical suspicion and, where needed, surgical exploration rather than a calculated score.

A 28-year-old woman with known asthma presents unable to complete sentences, peak flow 30% predicted, heart rate 128/min, respiratory rate 34/min, using accessory muscles, with a silent chest on auscultation and SpO2 89% on 15 L/min via non-rebreathe mask.

Structured questions

  1. What is your differential for this deterioration, and how do you investigate it?
  2. Assuming delayed cerebral ischaemia (DCI)/vasospasm is confirmed, outline your management.
  3. What is the evidence for nimodipine, and by what mechanism does it help?
  4. Why is "triple-H therapy" no longer recommended as previously taught?
Reveal model answer
  • Differential for deterioration at day 6 (classic DCI/vasospasm window, day 4–14): delayed cerebral ischaemia/vasospasm, rebleeding, hydrocephalus, seizure (including non-convulsive), metabolic derangement. Investigate with urgent CT head (rebleed, ventricular size), CTA or transcranial Doppler for vasospasm, EEG if seizure suspected, and basic metabolic screen.
  • Management of confirmed DCI: maintain euvolaemia (avoid hypovolaemia), induce hypertension with vasopressors if the aneurysm is secured and symptoms are attributable to DCI, and consider endovascular rescue therapy (intra-arterial vasodilators, balloon angioplasty) for refractory cases.
  • Nimodipine 60 mg enterally every 4 hours for 21 days improves neurological outcome and reduces secondary ischaemia — this benefit is thought to be substantially neuroprotective rather than purely due to reversing large-vessel vasospasm, which is why it is given prophylactically to all patients with aneurysmal SAH regardless of vasospasm status.
  • "Triple-H therapy" (hypertension, hypervolaemia, haemodilution) is outdated: the hypervolaemia and haemodilution components lack outcome evidence and carry real risk (pulmonary oedema, cardiac strain, reduced oxygen-carrying capacity). Current practice supports euvolaemia plus induced hypertension only.
Marking guide

20 marks: comprehensive, correctly-prioritised differential and workup (5), correct DCI management (euvolaemia + induced hypertension ± endovascular rescue) (6), accurate nimodipine mechanism/evidence (4), correct critique of triple-H therapy (5).

Guideline-drift trap: "Triple-H therapy" remains in some older recall material as standard DCI management. Current guidelines support euvolaemia and induced hypertension, but not deliberate hypervolaemia or haemodilution, which lack evidence of benefit and add cardiopulmonary risk.

CCS_S07 — Neutropenic Sepsis: Empirical Therapy and Time-Critical Management

Short case Sepsis / Infection CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4 20 marks · 5 min read / 12–13 min answer

Opening vignette

A 34-year-old man, day 10 post-chemotherapy for acute myeloid leukaemia, develops fever (38.9°C) and rigors. BP is 90/55 mmHg. Neutrophil count is 0.1 ×109/L.

Structured questions

  1. Define neutropenic sepsis and state your time-critical priority.
  2. What empirical antimicrobial therapy would you start, and when (if at all) would you add a glycopeptide?
  3. What other specific measures would you consider (growth factors, antifungals, source review)?
  4. How and when would you de-escalate therapy?
Reveal model answer
  • Neutropenic sepsis: fever ≥38°C with a neutrophil count ≤0.5 ×109/L (or expected to fall to this level). The critical priority is antibiotics within 1 hour of presentation — delay is strongly associated with increased mortality.
  • Empirical monotherapy with an anti-pseudomonal beta-lactam (piperacillin-tazobactam, or a carbapenem if higher resistance risk) is first-line per NICE/IDSA guidance. Add a glycopeptide (vancomycin/teicoplanin) only for specific indications — suspected line infection, severe mucositis, skin/soft tissue infection, haemodynamic instability, or known MRSA colonisation — not routinely for every presentation.
  • Review likely sources (central line, mucositis/gut translocation, perianal skin); G-CSF is not routinely recommended for treating established neutropenic sepsis (its role is mainly prophylactic in selected high-risk regimens); consider empirical antifungal therapy if fever persists ≥4–7 days despite broad-spectrum antibiotics in prolonged high-risk neutropenia, guided by galactomannan and CT imaging.
  • Reassess at 48–72 hours with culture results: de-escalate or switch to oral therapy in low-risk, clinically stable, afebrile patients (e.g. using the MASCC risk index); duration is guided by clinical response and neutrophil recovery rather than a fixed course length.
Marking guide

20 marks: correct definition and 1-hour antibiotic priority (5), correct monotherapy choice with specific (not routine) glycopeptide indications (6), sensible source review/G-CSF/antifungal reasoning (5), appropriate de-escalation approach (4).

Common error / drift trap: Reflexively adding vancomycin/a glycopeptide to "cover everything" in every neutropenic sepsis presentation is incorrect. Current guidelines reserve routine glycopeptide addition for specific indications, since unselected routine use increases resistance without proven outcome benefit.
