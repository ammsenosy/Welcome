How to use this resource

This bank contains 200 EDIC Part II–style stations:

  • 50 CCS (20 long, 30 short)
  • 150 CBS (50 imaging, 50 curves/waveforms, 50 biochemistry/data)

All stations are consultant-level, aligned with the EDIC Guidelines 2023–2026 and the CoBaTrICE syllabus.

Suggested approach

CCS stations

  • Long cases: allow yourself 10 minutes to read the opening vignette, then 25 minutes to answer out loud in structured bullet points, as in the real exam.
  • Short cases: 5 minutes reading, 12–13 minutes answering.
  • Answer out loud first, then write your bullet points. If you cannot say it in structured order in 60 seconds, you do not truly have it.
  • Before revealing the model answer, set your confidence: Confident / Unsure / Guessing. Do not adjust it afterwards.
  • Then reveal the model answer and mark yourself as an examiner would. Partial credit is allowed; benefit of the doubt is not.

CBS stations

  • Treat each as a 12-minute station with 8–12 expected answers.
  • Go through the images/curves/data in order, speaking your answers out loud, then write them down.
  • Set confidence before revealing, then mark yourself strictly.

Tracking your progress

  • For each station, record: confidence, marks awarded, and total marks.
  • Periodically review your performance by domain (respiratory, cardiovascular, neuro, etc.) to identify weak areas.
  • Re-sit selected stations after 2–4 weeks without re-reading the model answers first; the delta is the only measure that matters.

Blueprint coverage

  • The distribution of stations follows the EDIC Part II blueprint derived from CoBaTrICE:
    • Heavy emphasis on respiratory, cardiovascular, neurology, and infection.
    • Substantial coverage of renal/metabolic, GI/hepatic, haematology/oncology, endocrine/metabolic-toxic, obstetric, trauma, and end-of-life/ethics.
  • CBS stations emphasise data interpretation (imaging, curves, biochemistry) across all systems.

Use this bank as a calibration and gap-diagnostic tool, not just a question list. The goal is to expose guideline drift, strengthen structured verbal answers, and build exam-ready fluency.

CCS_L01 — Severe ARDS with Consideration of ECMO

Long case Respiratory CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4·6·7·11·12 40 marks · 10 min read / 25 min answer

Opening vignette

A 34-year-old previously fit man is admitted to ICU with severe community-acquired pneumonia. He is now on day 2 of invasive mechanical ventilation. Current settings: volume-control, tidal volume 6 mL/kg predicted body weight, PEEP 14 cmH2O, FiO2 1.0. Plateau pressure 32 cmH2O, driving pressure 18 cmH2O. Arterial blood gas: pH 7.29, PaCO2 6.8 kPa (51 mmHg), PaO2 9.0 kPa (68 mmHg) → PaO2/FiO2 ratio ≈ 68 mmHg. Chest X-ray shows bilateral diffuse airspace opacification. He is on noradrenaline 0.15 micrograms/kg/min, lactate 2.1 mmol/L, and a bedside echo shows preserved LV and RV function with no significant valvular disease.

Structured questions

  1. Part A — Assessment and diagnosis (10 min). How do you define and classify this patient's condition? What is your differential diagnosis for bilateral pulmonary infiltrates with severe hypoxaemia, and how would you exclude the main alternative diagnosis at the bedside?
  2. Part B — Immediate ventilatory management (10 min). Outline your lung-protective ventilation strategy and the evidence base for it. What adjuncts would you consider next, and on what evidence?
  3. Part C — Deterioration (5 min). Despite 16 hours of prone positioning, his PaO2/FiO2 ratio remains 65 mmHg, PaCO2 rises to 9.3 kPa (70 mmHg) and pH falls to 7.15 on unchanged lung-protective settings. What are your options now, and how would you decide whether to refer for veno-venous ECMO?
  4. Part D — Communication and ethics. The regional ECMO centre declines transfer, citing 9 days of high-pressure ventilation and evolving multi-organ dysfunction. How do you communicate this to the family, and what does your subsequent management plan look like?
Reveal model answer

Part A — Assessment and diagnosis

  • Apply the 2023 Global Definition of ARDS (Matthay et al., which updates and supersedes the 2012 Berlin Definition): onset within 1 week of a known clinical insult or new/ worsening respiratory symptoms; bilateral opacities not fully explained by effusion, lobar/lung collapse, or nodules — now explicitly on chest X-ray, CT, or lung ultrasound (Berlin recognised only X-ray/CT); respiratory failure not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload; and an oxygenation criterion that no longer requires invasive ventilation or even NIV/CPAP — patients on high-flow nasal oxygen ≥30 L/min can now be diagnosed with ARDS (PaO2/FiO2 ≤300 mmHg, or SpO2/FiO2 ≤315 with SpO2 ≤97% where an arterial gas is unavailable, e.g. in resource-limited settings). For intubated patients the familiar severity bands are retained on ≥5 cmH2O PEEP: mild PaO2/FiO2 201–300 mmHg, moderate 101–200 mmHg, severe ≤100 mmHg.
  • This patient meets criteria for severe ARDS (P/F ≈ 68 mmHg) secondary to severe CAP under both the Berlin and 2023 Global definitions, since she is already intubated and ventilated — but the distinction matters for less severe or non-intubated patients, who Berlin would have excluded from an ARDS diagnosis entirely.
  • Differentials for bilateral infiltrates with severe hypoxaemia: cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, diffuse alveolar haemorrhage, acute eosinophilic pneumonia, acute interstitial pneumonia, aspiration, transfusion- related lung injury (if relevant history), miliary TB/fungal disease in the right epidemiological context.
  • At the bedside: focused echocardiography (LV/RV function, filling, valves — already reassuring here), clinical trajectory/sepsis source, consider NT-proBNP if diagnostic uncertainty persists, review fluid balance and CVP/extravascular lung water if available; bronchoscopy with BAL if atypical course or immunocompromise.

Part B — Immediate ventilatory management

  • Lung-protective ventilation: tidal volume 4–8 mL/kg predicted body weight (target 6 mL/kg), plateau pressure <30 cmH2O, driving pressure as low as achievable (ideally <15 cmH2O — this patient's driving pressure of 18 is already a red flag), permissive hypercapnia accepted if pH tolerated (ARDSNet/ARMA trial, NEJM 2000).
  • PEEP: titrate using a higher-PEEP strategy for moderate–severe ARDS (e.g. ARDSNet high-PEEP/FiO2 table, or decremental PEEP trial guided by compliance/driving pressure); avoid PEEP that worsens driving pressure or causes haemodynamic compromise.
  • Prone positioning for ≥16 hours/day is indicated now (P/F <150 mmHg) — the PROSEVA trial (Guérin et al., NEJM 2013) showed a 28-day and 90-day mortality benefit with early, prolonged proning in severe ARDS. This should be instituted promptly, not held in reserve as a rescue therapy.
  • Neuromuscular blockade: consider a short period of neuromuscular blockade if there is patient–ventilator dyssynchrony or breath-stacking despite optimised sedation, rather than as a routine 48-hour infusion for every patient with P/F <150 (see drift trap below).
  • Conservative fluid management once shock resolved (FACTT trial principles); avoid unnecessary positive fluid balance.
  • Ensure adequate sedation/analgesia, lung-protective settings maintained even during transport or procedures, and daily reassessment of prone/supine cycling, weaning readiness and driving pressure.

Part C — Deterioration and ECMO referral

  • First re-confirm optimisation is genuine: adequate proning duration/technique, PEEP/recruitment appropriately titrated, sedation/neuromuscular blockade adequate, rule out a new complication (pneumothorax, mucus plugging, worsening infection, new cardiac dysfunction).
  • Consider a trial of inhaled pulmonary vasodilator (nitric oxide or prostacyclin) as a bridge — improves oxygenation transiently in a subset, no proven mortality benefit, useful while arranging transfer or managing severe hypoxaemia/RV strain.
  • This patient now has refractory hypoxaemia and hypercapnic acidosis despite optimised lung-protective ventilation and proning — a standard indication to refer for veno-venous ECMO, per criteria informed by the EOLIA trial (Combes et al., NEJM 2018): P/F <50 mmHg for >3 h, P/F <80 mmHg for >6 h, or pH <7.25 with PaCO2 ≥60 mmHg for >6 h despite optimised care.
  • Be able to discuss EOLIA honestly: the trial's primary 60-day mortality outcome was not statistically significant (relative risk 0.76, 95% CI 0.55–1.04), but it was stopped early for futility/safety boundary crossing, and 28% of the control arm crossed over to ECMO as rescue — this materially confounds interpretation. Post-hoc Bayesian re-analysis and individual-patient-data meta-analyses (including CESAR) suggest a likely mortality benefit in patients meeting these severity criteria.
  • Refer early rather than late — outcomes worsen with each additional day of high-pressure/high-FiO2 ventilation before cannulation; discuss with the regional ECMO centre before the patient is unsalvageable, and arrange safe mobile ECMO retrieval if accepted.
  • Know the relative contraindications (ELSO guidance): prolonged high-pressure ventilation (typically >7–10 days), significant pre-existing CNS injury with poor prognosis, uncontrolled multi-organ failure, severe irreversible comorbidity, and severe immunosuppression — these are relative and considered in the context of the whole patient, not absolute cut-offs.

Part D — Communication and ongoing management if ECMO declined

  • Communicate honestly and compassionately: explain what has been tried, why ECMO was considered, and why the receiving centre felt it was unlikely to change the outcome given prolonged high-pressure ventilation and evolving organ failure — avoid false reassurance or minimising the severity.
  • Use a structured approach (e.g. SPIKES or equivalent), ensure a senior clinician leads the discussion, check understanding, allow silence and questions, and document the conversation.
  • Re-focus the management plan on optimising every reversible element of conventional therapy: continued lung-protective ventilation, proning, neuromuscular blockade if dyssynchronous, treatment of the underlying infection, nutrition, and daily review of organ support.
  • Proactively revisit goals of care with the family as the multidisciplinary team's assessment evolves; if the trajectory continues to worsen despite maximal conventional therapy, initiate early, structured discussions about ceilings of care and, if appropriate, a shift to comfort-focused care — this is not a failure of critical care but part of it.
  • Ensure consistency of messaging across the team, offer psychological/spiritual support, and document a clear escalation/de-escalation plan for the out-of-hours team.
Marking guide

Part A (10 marks): full definition with correct mention of the 2023 Global Definition update (4), correct severity classification (2), at least 3 relevant differentials (2), a bedside method to exclude cardiogenic oedema (2).

Part B (10 marks): correct tidal volume/plateau/driving pressure targets (3), PEEP strategy (2), timely proning with correct evidence (3), appropriately nuanced neuromuscular blockade answer (2 — see drift trap).

Part C (10 marks): systematic exclusion of reversible causes before escalating (2), mention of inhaled pulmonary vasodilator as bridge (1), correct EOLIA-based referral criteria (4), balanced critique of EOLIA evidence (2), timeliness of referral (1).

Part D (10 marks): structured breaking-bad-news approach (3), avoidance of false reassurance (2), concrete ongoing management plan (3), proactive goals-of-care planning (2).

Guideline-drift trap: Older recall material teaches "routine 48-hour cisatracurium infusion for all patients with P/F <150" based on the ACURASYS trial (Papazian et al., NEJM 2010). The subsequent ROSE trial (National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute PETAL Network, NEJM 2019) compared early continuous neuromuscular blockade with deep sedation against a strategy of light sedation with as-needed neuromuscular blockade, and found no difference in 90-day mortality — the trial was stopped for futility. Current best practice (and current ESICM/ATS guidance) is to reserve neuromuscular blockade for patients with ongoing dyssynchrony, breath-stacking, or refractory hypoxaemia despite optimised sedation, rather than applying it routinely and continuously to every patient meeting the ACURASYS oxygenation threshold. Candidates who state ACURASYS as current unqualified practice without acknowledging ROSE should lose marks.
Guideline-drift trap: Quoting only the 2012 Berlin Definition as if it were current, unqualified practice is itself a drift trap examiners now watch for. The 2023 Global Definition of ARDS (Matthay et al.) updated Berlin in three substantive ways: (1) it permits diagnosis in patients on high-flow nasal oxygen (≥30 L/min) rather than requiring invasive ventilation or NIV/CPAP ≥5 cmH2O — meaningfully widening who can be labelled ARDS and enrolled/managed as such; (2) it accepts lung ultrasound as an imaging modality alongside chest X-ray/CT; (3) it allows SpO2/FiO2 to substitute for PaO2/FiO2 when an arterial line/gas is unavailable, particularly relevant in resource-limited settings. Candidates citing Berlin alone, with no acknowledgement that it has been superseded, should lose marks — this is precisely the kind of citation-currency check the exam expects.

CCS_S01 — Septic Shock: Vasopressor Escalation and Steroid Decisions

Short case Sepsis / Infection CoBaTrICE 1·3·4·11 20 marks · 5 min read / 12–13 min answer

Opening vignette

A 58-year-old woman is admitted with septic shock secondary to a perforated sigmoid diverticulum, now 6 hours post source-control laparotomy. She has received 30 mL/kg of balanced crystalloid and is on noradrenaline 0.6 micrograms/kg/min with a MAP of 60 mmHg. Lactate is 4.8 mmol/L (was 6.2 mmol/L on admission). She is anuric for the last 2 hours despite a normal preoperative creatinine.

Structured questions

  1. Summarise your immediate assessment and resuscitation priorities at this point.
  2. Her noradrenaline requirement continues to climb. What is your approach to second-agent vasopressor therapy, and what is the evidence for your choice?
  3. Would you start corticosteroids? Justify your answer with reference to the trial evidence, including where the evidence is inconsistent.
  4. How would you monitor response and plan de-escalation over the next 24–48 hours?
Reveal model answer

1. Immediate assessment and priorities

  • Confirm source control has been effective (drain output, imaging if concern), send repeat cultures if not already done, review antimicrobial choice/spectrum and de-escalate per culture data when available.
  • Reassess volume status dynamically (passive leg raise, pulse pressure variation if ventilated and appropriate, echo) rather than giving further boluses reflexively — she has already received 30 mL/kg and is oliguric/anuric, which may reflect AKI rather than pure hypovolaemia.
  • Target MAP ≥65 mmHg (individualise upward if chronically hypertensive); trend lactate (already falling, a reassuring sign) rather than chasing a normal value acutely.
  • Assess for a surgical or mechanical complication if trajectory doesn't fit (anastomotic leak, ongoing contamination, abdominal compartment syndrome).

2. Second-agent vasopressor therapy

  • Noradrenaline remains first-line; at doses in the range of 0.25–0.5 micrograms/kg/min with ongoing hypotension, current Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidance suggests adding vasopressin rather than simply escalating noradrenaline further.
  • Evidence base: the VASST trial (Russell et al., NEJM 2008) found no overall mortality difference between vasopressin and noradrenaline, though a hypothesis-generating subgroup with less severe shock appeared to benefit. Vasopressin's practical advantages are a catecholamine- sparing effect and possibly less tachyarrhythmia.
  • If shock remains refractory despite noradrenaline plus vasopressin, adrenaline can be added as a third agent; consider hydrocortisone (see below) and reassess for an unrecognised cause (adrenal insufficiency, cardiac dysfunction, ongoing source, acidaemia impairing catecholamine responsiveness).

3. Corticosteroids

  • Yes — this patient, with an escalating noradrenaline requirement well above 0.25 micrograms/kg/min, meets a reasonable threshold to start IV hydrocortisone 200 mg/day (as a continuous infusion or in divided doses).
  • Evidence is genuinely mixed and candidates should say so: CORTICUS (Sprung et al., NEJM 2008) showed no survival benefit from hydrocortisone regardless of ACTH-stimulation response, but faster shock reversal. ADRENAL (Venkatesh et al., NEJM 2018) — hydrocortisone alone — showed faster resolution of shock but no 90-day mortality benefit. APROCCHSS (Annane et al., NEJM 2018) — hydrocortisone plus fludrocortisone — did show a 90-day all-cause mortality benefit.
  • The discrepancy between ADRENAL (hydrocortisone alone, no mortality signal) and APROCCHSS (hydrocortisone + fludrocortisone, mortality benefit) is unresolved; the Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 gives a weak recommendation for IV corticosteroids in adults with septic shock and an ongoing vasopressor requirement, without mandating fludrocortisone co-administration everywhere. Local protocol should be followed, and this uncertainty should be explicitly acknowledged rather than presented as settled.

4. Monitoring and de-escalation

  • Serial lactate clearance, trend MAP/vasopressor dose, urine output and creatinine (renal recovery vs need for RRT), and clinical markers of perfusion — capillary refill time is a reasonable adjunct (ANDROMEDA-SHOCK, Hernández et al., JAMA 2019: CRT-targeted resuscitation trended toward better outcomes than lactate-targeted resuscitation, though the primary endpoint was not statistically significant in the frequentist analysis).
  • Avoid ongoing large-volume fluid administration once shock is stabilising — a restrictive fluid strategy after initial resuscitation has not shown harm and may reduce fluid overload (CLASSIC trial, Meyhoff et al., NEJM 2022: restrictive vs liberal IV fluids in septic shock, no significant difference in 90-day mortality, supporting a more conservative approach as reasonable).
  • Wean vasopressors as MAP is maintained on falling doses; taper hydrocortisone rather than stopping abruptly once vasopressors are off; reassess antimicrobial duration/de-escalation daily; screen for new organ dysfunction (AKI progression, ileus, ICU-acquired weakness) as she recovers.
Marking guide

Q1 (5 marks): dynamic (not reflexive) fluid assessment (2), source-control/antimicrobial review (1), correct MAP target and lactate-trend reasoning (2).

Q2 (5 marks): correct threshold/rationale for adding vasopressin (3), accurate VASST summary (2).

Q3 (5 marks): correct decision to start hydrocortisone with threshold (2), accurate, balanced summary of CORTICUS/ADRENAL/APROCCHSS (3 — see drift trap).

Q4 (5 marks): appropriate monitoring parameters (2), restrictive fluid strategy post-resuscitation (2), safe weaning plan (1).

Guideline-drift trap: Many recall-based answers still state that fludrocortisone is a standard, unconditional addition to hydrocortisone in septic shock. In fact, ADRENAL (hydrocortisone alone) showed no mortality benefit while APROCCHSS (hydrocortisone plus fludrocortisone) did — the two major contemporary trials used different regimens and reached different mortality conclusions. Examiners should expect candidates to acknowledge this inconsistency explicitly rather than quoting a single trial as if it were the whole picture.

CCS_L02 — Hypercapnic COPD Exacerbation: NIV, Ventilation Strategy and Tracheostomy Decision-Making

Long case Respiratory CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4·5·7·11 40 marks · 10 min read / 25 min answer

Opening vignette

A 68-year-old woman with known COPD (FEV1 38% predicted, on home oxygen) presents with an infective exacerbation. Arterial blood gas on controlled 28% oxygen: pH 7.28, PaCO2 6.8 kPa (51 mmHg), PaO2 7.9 kPa (59 mmHg), bicarbonate 28 mmol/L. She is started on non-invasive ventilation (NIV) in the emergency department alongside nebulised bronchodilators, steroids and antibiotics.

Structured questions

  1. Part A (10 min). What are your indications for starting NIV here, your initial settings, and how will you monitor for early success or failure?
  2. Part B (10 min). After 2 hours, she is more drowsy; repeat gas shows pH 7.19, PaCO2 8.9 kPa (67 mmHg). What do you do now, and if intubated, how does your ventilation strategy differ from a patient without airflow obstruction?
  3. Part C (5 min). By day 10 she remains ventilator-dependent with repeated failed spontaneous breathing trials. Outline your weaning approach and your plan for extubation.
  4. Part D. Weaning continues to fail. How do you approach the tracheostomy decision, and what wider issues does this raise for this patient?
Reveal model answer

Part A — NIV initiation and monitoring

  • Indications: acute hypercapnic respiratory failure with respiratory acidosis (pH <7.35, PaCO2 >6 kPa) persisting despite optimal medical therapy — met here (pH 7.28).
  • Initial settings: pressure-support/bilevel mode, start EPAP ~4–5 cmH2O and IPAP ~10–12 cmH2O, titrate IPAP upward in increments to reduce work of breathing and PaCO2, target SpO2 88–92% (controlled oxygen, avoid hyperoxia-driven hypercapnia).
  • Monitor: repeat ABG at 1 hour is the key predictor of success/failure (BTS/RCP audit-based practice); also track respiratory rate, consciousness level, accessory muscle use, and patient tolerance/synchrony continuously.
  • Early failure predictors: worsening pH/PaCO2 or unchanged acidosis at 1 hour, falling GCS, haemodynamic instability, inability to tolerate the interface — these should prompt early escalation planning (ceiling-of-care discussion and/or intubation readiness) rather than persisting with NIV alone.

Part B — NIV failure and intubation strategy

  • This is NIV failure (worsening acidosis and consciousness despite adequate trial) — proceed to intubation and invasive ventilation unless a pre-agreed ceiling of care precludes this; do not persist with NIV in a deteriorating, drowsy patient.
  • Ventilation strategy differs from a non-obstructed patient principally around avoiding dynamic hyperinflation: use a lower respiratory rate, prolong expiratory time (adjust I:E ratio, e.g. 1:3–1:4), accept a higher inspiratory flow to shorten inspiratory time, and permit permissive hypercapnia (target pH >7.20–7.25 rather than normocapnia).
  • Set extrinsic PEEP cautiously to roughly 70–85% of measured intrinsic PEEP once the patient is triggering spontaneously, to offset trigger workload — but not routinely in a fully controlled, paralysed patient (same principle as CBS_CRV_01).
  • Continue aggressive bronchodilation, treat the underlying trigger (infection), and monitor plateau pressure and measured auto-PEEP rather than peak pressure alone.

Part C — Weaning approach

  • Daily assessment of weaning readiness: resolving precipitant, adequate oxygenation (P/F >150–200 on modest support), haemodynamic stability, adequate cough/secretion clearance, awake and cooperative.
  • Perform a structured spontaneous breathing trial (SBT) once readiness criteria are met (e.g. low-level pressure support or T-piece for 30–120 minutes), assessing rapid shallow breathing index, work of breathing, gas exchange and haemodynamics.
  • Consider a diaphragm-protective approach — avoid both over-assistance (disuse atrophy) and under-assistance (fatigue) during the weaning phase; treat reversible contributors to weaning failure (electrolyte derangement, fluid overload, undernutrition, ICU-acquired weakness, undertreated bronchospasm).
  • If extubation is attempted, plan for a high-risk extubation given underlying COPD: consider planned post-extubation NIV to prevent reintubation (see evidence below), have a clear reintubation trigger plan.

Part D — Tracheostomy decision and wider issues

  • Tracheostomy is considered for anticipated prolonged ventilator dependence, to improve comfort, reduce sedation requirement, facilitate weaning trials and rehabilitation, and improve secretion clearance — not as a default step after a fixed number of failed extubations.
  • Timing: the TracMan trial (Young et al., JAMA 2013) found no significant difference in 30-day mortality, ICU length of stay, or 2-year mortality between early (within 4 days) and late (after 10 days) tracheostomy — timing should be individualised rather than protocolised to "early is always better."
  • Given her severe underlying COPD and now prolonged, difficult-to-wean respiratory failure, this is also the point to proactively revisit goals of care with the patient (if able to participate) and family: realistic long-term prognosis, likelihood of ventilator liberation, quality of life with possible long-term tracheostomy/home ventilation, and her own previously expressed wishes.
  • Involve respiratory medicine, physiotherapy, and palliative care early in parallel with ongoing active treatment — these are not mutually exclusive, and early integration improves decision quality and family experience.
Marking guide

Part A (10): correct indications/thresholds (3), sensible initial settings (2), correct emphasis on 1-hour gas as key predictor (3), appropriate failure criteria (2).

CCS_L03 — Cardiogenic Shock Complicating Acute MI: Inotropes and Mechanical Circulatory Support

Long case Cardiovascular CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4·5·11·12 40 marks · 10 min read / 25 min answer

Opening vignette

A 62-year-old man is admitted to ICU three hours after primary PCI to the LAD for an anterior STEMI. He is now hypotensive (BP 78/50 mmHg), cool and mottled peripherally, with a rising lactate (5.2 mmol/L) and falling urine output. He is on dobutamine and noradrenaline. Bedside echocardiography shows a severely impaired LV (EF ~20%) with no significant mechanical complication (no VSD, no significant MR, no tamponade) and a normally functioning RV.

Structured questions

  1. Part A (7 min). How do you define and stage this patient's shock state, and what is your systematic bedside assessment?
  2. Part B (8 min). Critique his current pharmacological support and justify your preferred inotrope/vasopressor strategy.
  3. Part C (7 min). Despite optimised pharmacological therapy he remains in refractory shock. Discuss your approach to mechanical circulatory support, including the current evidence for each option.
  4. Part D (3 min). How do you structure the ongoing multidisciplinary and family communication around this decision?
Reveal model answer

Part A — Definition, staging, assessment

  • Cardiogenic shock: primary cardiac dysfunction causing tissue hypoperfusion, typically with hypotension, elevated filling pressures, and end-organ hypoperfusion (oliguria, lactate, altered mentation) not explained by hypovolaemia alone.
  • Stage using the SCAI shock classification (A "at risk" → E "extremis"); this patient — hypotensive, hypoperfused, on two agents but not yet deteriorating to refractory arrest/multi-organ collapse — is broadly SCAI stage C ("classic" cardiogenic shock), trending toward D if worsening despite escalating support.
  • Bedside assessment: focused echo (chamber size/function, valve pathology, pericardial effusion, RV function), invasive arterial line, consider central venous/cardiac output monitoring, 12-lead ECG and repeat troponin to exclude ongoing ischaemia/mechanical complication, review the PCI result for stent patency concerns.

Part B — Pharmacological strategy

  • Norepinephrine remains the preferred first-line vasopressor in cardiogenic shock to maintain coronary/organ perfusion pressure; dobutamine is a reasonable inotrope choice for augmenting cardiac output in a vasopressor-supported patient with preserved-enough blood pressure.
  • Avoid epinephrine as first-line where alternatives are adequate — it is associated with higher lactate, more tachyarrhythmia, and signals of worse metabolic/renal outcomes compared with norepinephrine–dobutamine combinations in comparative studies of cardiogenic shock.
  • Reassess volume status before escalating vasoactive drugs further — under- or over-filling both worsen cardiac output in severe LV dysfunction; titrate to a clear haemodynamic target (MAP ≥65 mmHg, improving lactate/urine output) rather than an arbitrary dose ceiling.

Part C — Mechanical circulatory support

  • Intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP): the IABP-SHOCK II trial (Thiele et al., NEJM 2012, with consistent long-term follow-up) showed no mortality benefit from routine IABP in AMI-cardiogenic shock — it is no longer recommended as routine therapy, though may still have a role for specific mechanical complications or as an adjunct in select cases.
  • Percutaneous microaxial LV support (e.g. Impella CP): the DanGer Shock trial (Møller et al., NEJM 2024) showed a significant reduction in 180-day mortality with early Impella CP versus standard care in AMI-cardiogenic shock, but at the cost of significantly higher rates of adverse events — major bleeding, limb ischaemia, and need for renal replacement therapy. This represents genuinely new, practice-relevant evidence.
  • VA-ECMO: the ECLS-SHOCK trial (Thiele et al., NEJM 2023) — VA-ECMO plus usual care versus usual care alone in infarct-related cardiogenic shock with planned revascularisation — found no significant difference in 30-day all-cause mortality, with more bleeding and peripheral vascular complications in the ECMO arm. VA-ECMO retains a role for biventricular failure, refractory arrest, or as a bridge to decision/transplant/durable device, but is not a proven mortality-reducing default for AMI-cardiogenic shock.
  • Device choice should be individualised (LV-only vs biventricular failure, vascular access, institutional expertise, exit strategy) and discussed with a shock/MCS multidisciplinary team rather than applied reflexively.

Part D — Communication

  • Early, honest discussion with the family about the severity of shock, the specific evidence and trade-offs (mortality benefit vs complication risk) behind any proposed device, and realistic best/worst-case trajectories.
  • Involve cardiology, cardiac surgery/MCS team, and critical care jointly; document a clear escalation ceiling and a plan for de-escalation/withdrawal if the chosen support fails to reverse shock or if complications (e.g. limb ischaemia, major bleeding, irreversible multi-organ failure) supervene.

The current trial evidence is more nuanced and genuinely contradicts this: IABP shows no benefit, DanGer Shock shows a mortality benefit for Impella CP specifically but with materially higher complication rates, and ECLS-SHOCK shows VA-ECMO does not reduce mortality despite intuitive physiological appeal. Device-specific, trial-based reasoning is required rather than a general "more support is better" heuristic.

CCS_L04 — Severe Traumatic Brain Injury: ICP/CPP Management and Prognostication

Long case Neurology / Neurocritical Care CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4·5·7·8·11·12 40 marks · 10 min read / 25 min answer

Opening vignette

A 24-year-old man sustains a severe traumatic brain injury in a high-speed road traffic collision (GCS 3 at scene, now intubated). CT head shows bifrontal contusions and diffuse axonal injury with 4 mm midline shift and effaced basal cisterns. An intraparenchymal ICP monitor is inserted; opening pressure is 28 mmHg.

Structured questions

  1. Part A (8 min). Outline your first-tier goals and therapies for managing raised ICP in the first hours.
  2. Part B (9 min). ICP remains >25 mmHg despite first-tier measures. Discuss your second-tier options and the evidence behind each.
  3. Part C (5 min). What role, if any, does multimodal neuromonitoring play beyond ICP and CPP targets?
  4. Part D (3 min). How do you approach prognostication and communication with the family over the following days?
Reveal model answer

Part A — First-tier management

  • Targets per the Brain Trauma Foundation 4th edition guidelines and the Seattle International Severe TBI Consensus Conference (SIBICC) algorithm: ICP <22 mmHg, CPP typically 60–70 mmHg (individualised, avoiding aggressive CPP augmentation beyond need).
  • First-tier measures: 30° head-up positioning with neutral neck alignment, adequate sedation and analgesia, normocapnia (avoid unintentional hyper- or hypocapnia), normothermia, avoidance of hyponatraemia/hypotonic fluids, treatment of seizures, and CSF drainage via EVD if in place.
  • Ensure adequate oxygenation and MAP to maintain CPP, and correct coagulopathy given contusional injury with expansion risk.

Part B — Second-tier options and evidence

  • Hyperosmolar therapy: mannitol or hypertonic saline, both reasonable — hypertonic saline may be preferred in hypovolaemic/hypotensive patients as it does not cause an osmotic diuresis; evidence does not clearly favour one over the other for outcome.
  • Barbiturate coma: can reduce refractory ICP but with weak outcome evidence and significant risk of hypotension/immunosuppression — reserved for truly refractory cases with haemodynamic support available.
  • Decompressive craniectomy: DECRA (Cooper et al., NEJM 2011) — early bifrontal craniectomy for diffuse injury reduced ICP but was associated with worse functional outcome. RESCUEicp (Hutchinson et al., NEJM 2016) — craniectomy for refractory ICP reduced mortality but with a higher proportion of survivors in a vegetative state or with severe disability; overall favourable outcome rates were similar between groups. This is not a straightforward "life-saving" intervention and must be framed to families as a genuine trade-off, not a rescue cure.
  • Therapeutic hypothermia: Eurotherm3235 (Andrews et al., NEJM 2015) — hypothermia as a second-tier ICP therapy was associated with worse outcomes than standard care and the trial was stopped early for harm. Hypothermia should not be used as a routine second-tier ICP therapy despite the physiological rationale.

Part C — Multimodal monitoring

  • Fixed ICP/CPP thresholds may not reflect individual autoregulatory status; adjuncts include brain tissue oxygen tension (PbtO2) monitoring to detect regional ischaemia despite "normal" ICP, cerebral microdialysis (lactate/pyruvate ratio, glucose) for metabolic crisis detection, and autoregulation indices (e.g. pressure reactivity index, PRx) to individualise optimal CPP rather than applying a fixed population target.
  • Evidence for outcome benefit from PbtO2-guided therapy is still maturing (e.g. BOOST-3) — candidates should present this as a promising adjunct for individualising care rather than a guideline-mandated standard.

Part D — Prognostication and communication

  • Avoid premature prognostication or withdrawal decisions within the first 72 hours — early clinical findings and even early imaging can both underestimate and overestimate eventual recovery potential, particularly in younger patients.
  • Use validated prediction models (IMPACT, CRASH) as adjuncts to, not replacements for, clinical judgement and serial reassessment; communicate uncertainty honestly with the family.
  • Maintain regular, structured, multidisciplinary family updates; if the trajectory suggests catastrophic, non-survivable injury, initiate brain death testing per protocol when clinically appropriate and raise organ donation according to local pathways and the family's own wishes.

Long case Sepsis / Infection CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4·5·6·11·12 40 marks · 10 min read / 25 min answer

Opening vignette

A 45-year-old man with type 2 diabetes presents with rapidly progressive pain and swelling of the left leg, out of proportion to the visible findings, with scattered bullae. He is tachycardic (130/min), hypotensive (85/50 mmHg), lactate 6.5 mmol/L, white cell count 2.1 ×109/L (leucopenia), CRP 380 mg/L.

  1. Part A (8 min). What is your leading diagnosis, how confident are you, and what is the role (and limitations) of clinical scoring systems and imaging here?

  3. Part C (7 min). Discuss source control: timing, extent, and the evidence for adjunctive therapies that are sometimes proposed.
  4. Part D (3 min). What are the key considerations in his ongoing ICU course and communication with him/his family?
Reveal model answer

Part A — Diagnosis and the role of scores/imaging

  • This presentation — pain out of proportion, rapid progression, bullae, systemic toxicity with leucopenia (a marker of overwhelming infection) — is highly suspicious for a necrotising soft tissue infection (NSTI), a surgical emergency.
  • The LRINEC score can support suspicion but has poor sensitivity and was derived primarily as a rule-in, not a rule-out, tool — a low score must never be used to defer surgical exploration when clinical suspicion is high.
  • CT/MRI may show fascial oedema/gas but should not delay surgery in a clinically obvious case; definitive diagnosis is surgical — direct visualisation of grey, non-bleeding, easily dissecting ("finger test" positive) fascial planes.

Part B — Resuscitation and antimicrobials

  • Standard sepsis bundle: cultures (blood, and intraoperative tissue when source control occurs), balanced crystalloid resuscitation guided dynamically, vasopressors (norepinephrine) if MAP not achieved with fluids, early broad-spectrum antibiotics.
  • Empirical coverage for polymicrobial/toxin-producing organisms: broad-spectrum beta-lactam (piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem) plus clindamycin (for its antitoxin, protein-synthesis-inhibiting effect against toxin-producing streptococci/staphylococci), plus consideration of MRSA cover (vancomycin/linezolid) pending local epidemiology and risk factors.

Part C — Source control and adjuncts

  • Urgent surgical debridement is the single most important intervention — every hour of delay is associated with increased mortality; do not wait for imaging or haemodynamic "optimisation" to proceed to theatre once diagnosis is clinically established.
  • Radical debridement of all non-viable tissue, with planned second-look surgery at 24–48 hours (and repeated as needed) is standard; amputation may be required for limb-threatening/uncontrolled infection.
  • IV immunoglobulin: proposed as adjunctive therapy to neutralise circulating exotoxin, but the INSTINCT trial (Madsen et al.) found no significant benefit on SOFA score/mortality — its routine use is not supported by robust evidence and should not be presented as standard of care.
  • Hyperbaric oxygen: theoretical rationale, no robust RCT evidence of benefit, and must never delay surgical source control — logistically often impractical in an unstable patient.

Part D — Ongoing ICU course and communication

  • Anticipate a prolonged, catabolic critical illness: multi-organ support, aggressive nutrition (high protein/energy requirements), meticulous wound care between debridements, analgesia (often complex given repeated procedures), and early rehabilitation planning.
  • Address the psychological impact of major tissue loss/potential amputation and disfigurement early — involve psychology, plastics/reconstructive surgery, and rehabilitation medicine.
  • Maintain honest, structured communication with the patient (as he recovers capacity) and family about prognosis, the possibility of further surgery/amputation, and realistic functional expectations.

However, recent major surgery (hip replacement 3 days ago) is a significant relative bleeding-risk factor — this creates a genuine clinical tension between imminent risk of death from obstructive shock and major bleeding risk, requiring shared, senior/multidisciplinary decision-making rather than an automatic "never thrombolyse post-op" rule.

  • Start therapeutic anticoagulation (typically unfractionated heparin infusion acutely, for titratability) as soon as safely possible regardless of reperfusion strategy, unless active bleeding precludes it; transition to a DOAC or warfarin once stable, for a minimum of 3 months given a clear provoking factor (surgery), with duration extended if risk factors persist.
Marking guide

20 marks: correct high-risk PE classification (4), correct first-line reperfusion choice with explicit recognition of the surgical bleeding-risk tension (6), sensible alternative reperfusion strategies (6), appropriate anticoagulation plan and duration (4).

Common error / drift trap: Recent major surgery is often treated as an absolute contraindication to thrombolysis. CCS_L06 — Severe AKI with Hyperkalaemia: Timing and Modality of Renal Replacement Therapy

Long case Renal / Metabolic CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4·5·11 40 marks · 10 min read / 25 min answer

Opening vignette

A 72-year-old man with septic shock from urosepsis has been oliguric for 18 hours. Potassium is 6.8 mmol/L with ECG changes (peaked T waves, widening QRS). Creatinine has risen from 90 to 350 µmol/L over 48 hours. pH 7.22, bicarbonate 14 mmol/L, lactate 3.1 mmol/L. He remains on noradrenaline 0.3 micrograms/kg/min but is responding to ongoing resuscitation.

Structured questions

  1. Part A (8 min). Manage the hyperkalaemia in front of you right now.
  2. Part B (7 min). Classify his AKI and state whether this presentation mandates emergency RRT or whether medical management first is reasonable.
  3. Part C (7 min). More broadly, what does the current evidence say about the timing of RRT initiation in critically ill AKI without an emergency indication?
  4. Part D (3 min). If RRT is started, justify your modality, anticoagulation and dosing choices in this haemodynamically supported patient.
Reveal model answer

Part A — Immediate hyperkalaemia management

  • Stabilise the myocardium: IV calcium gluconate (or chloride) — this does not lower potassium but reduces the risk of malignant arrhythmia; repeat if ECG changes persist.
  • Shift potassium intracellularly: insulin-dextrose (e.g. 10 units soluble insulin with 25 g dextrose) and nebulised salbutamol — synergistic, onset 15–30 minutes, monitor for delayed hypoglycaemia.
  • Correct significant acidosis if present as part of the overall resuscitation, but bicarbonate is not a reliable stand-alone potassium-lowering therapy.
  • Definitive removal: given refractory hyperkalaemia with ECG changes and evolving oliguric AKI, this patient has an emergency indication for RRT — arrange urgently rather than relying on medical therapy alone to control potassium indefinitely.

Part B — AKI classification and indication for RRT

  • By KDIGO criteria, this is at least stage 2–3 AKI (creatinine rise, oliguria >12h).
  • He already meets a recognised emergency indication for RRT: refractory/severe hyperkalaemia with ECG changes not settling with medical therapy, in the context of oliguric AKI — this alone justifies proceeding to RRT now rather than waiting further.

Part C — Evidence on RRT timing without an emergency indication

  • For AKI without an emergency indication, the evidence has shifted away from routine early initiation: ELAIN (Zarbock et al., JAMA 2016), a small single-centre trial, suggested a survival benefit for early RRT, but this was not replicated in larger, multicentre trials.
  • AKIKI (Gaudry et al., NEJM 2016) and IDEAL-ICU (Barbar et al., NEJM 2018) found no mortality benefit for early RRT over a delayed strategy (initiating only when a clear indication developed).
  • STARRT-AKI (NEJM 2020) — the largest, most definitive trial — found no mortality benefit from an accelerated RRT strategy, and more RRT-dependence at 90 days and catheter-related adverse events/hypotension in the accelerated arm.
  • Current best practice: adopt a watchful-waiting strategy, initiating RRT when a clear indication develops (refractory hyperkalaemia, severe acidosis, diuretic-refractory fluid overload, uraemic complications) rather than routinely by AKI stage or creatinine trajectory alone.

Part D — Modality, anticoagulation, dosing

  • Modality: continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) is generally preferred over intermittent haemodialysis in haemodynamically unstable/vasopressor-dependent patients for haemodynamic tolerability, though intermittent therapy can be used with careful technique.
  • Anticoagulation: regional citrate anticoagulation is preferred over systemic heparin where available — the RICH trial (Zarbock et al., JAMA 2020) showed longer filter/circuit patency and less bleeding with citrate compared with heparin.
  • Dose: standard-intensity CRRT dosing (around 20–25 mL/kg/h effluent) is adequate — the RENAL (Bellomo et al., NEJM 2009) and ATN (VA/NIH, NEJM 2008) trials both showed no outcome benefit from higher-intensity dosing (e.g. 40 mL/kg/h) over standard dosing.

The larger, better-powered AKIKI, IDEAL-ICU and STARRT-AKI trials found no mortality benefit to early or accelerated initiation, with STARRT-AKI additionally showing harm signals (more RRT dependence, more catheter-related complications). Opening vignette

A 26-year-old woman is found with empty paracetamol packets and presents 36 hours post-ingestion with jaundice. INR 6.8, creatinine 180 µmol/L, pH 7.28, lactate 4.5 mmol/L, ammonia elevated. She is confused but rousable — grade III hepatic encephalopathy.

  2. Part B (8 min). Outline your specific management, including the role and timing of N-acetylcysteine and your approach to correcting the coagulopathy.
  3. Part C (7 min). Apply the King's College Criteria to this patient and discuss their reliability.
  4. Part D (3 min). Outline the transplant-listing process and the wider issues this case raises.
Reveal model answer

Part A — Diagnosis and grading

  • Acute liver failure (ALF) is defined as coagulopathy (INR >1.5) with any degree of encephalopathy in a patient without pre-existing liver disease, within 26 weeks of illness onset — met here.
  • Grade encephalopathy using the West Haven criteria; grade III (marked confusion, incoherent, but rousable) carries a significant risk of progression to grade IV (coma) and cerebral oedema.

Part B — Specific management

  • N-acetylcysteine (NAC) should be given even this late, and even with uncertain ingestion history — NAC remains indicated in established paracetamol-induced ALF beyond the usual 24-hour treatment window because it has benefits beyond glutathione repletion (antioxidant, microcirculatory, and possibly outcome benefits in ALF itself).

  • Manage cerebral oedema risk: 30° head-up positioning, avoid hyponatraemia (target high-normal sodium), consider hypertonic saline, treat seizures aggressively, and monitor trend of ammonia and neurological status closely; invasive ICP monitoring is used selectively (given bleeding risk from coagulopathy) rather than routinely.

  • Paracetamol-specific King's College Criteria (KCC): transplant is indicated if pH <7.25 (after adequate resuscitation, >24h post-ingestion) OR all three of: INR >6.5 (PT >100 s), creatinine >300 µmol/L, and grade III/IV encephalopathy.
  • Applying this patient's values: pH 7.28 does not meet the acidosis criterion; of the triad, INR 6.8 (>6.5 ✓) and grade III encephalopathy (✓) are met, but creatinine 180 µmol/L is not >300 µmol/L — so she does not strictly meet the full KCC triad at this snapshot, despite being clearly high-risk.
  • KCC has only moderate sensitivity (widely reported in the range of ~60–70%) — a proportion of patients who will die without transplant do not meet the criteria at any single time point. Trajectory (worsening encephalopathy grade, rising lactate, failure of lactate to clear) matters as much as a single snapshot, and urgent transplant-centre discussion should not be withheld simply because the full criteria are not yet met.

Part D — Transplant listing and wider issues

  • Discuss urgently with the regional liver transplant/super-urgent listing service regardless of exact criteria met, given her trajectory and high-risk features; know the broad contraindications to listing (uncontrolled sepsis, refractory shock, irreversible severe neurological injury).
  • Address capacity issues — hepatic encephalopathy impairs her ability to consent, requiring best-interests decision-making with family involvement.
  • Given the self-harm context, plan safeguarding assessment and psychiatric review once she has recovered capacity, alongside honest, sensitive family communication throughout.

CCS_L08 — Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura vs DIC: Diagnosis and Plasma Exchange

Long case Haematology / Oncology CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4·11 40 marks · 10 min read / 25 min answer

Opening vignette

A 38-year-old woman presents with fever, confusion, and petechiae. Haemoglobin 68 g/L, platelets 12 ×109/L, schistocytes on blood film, LDH markedly elevated, haptoglobin undetectable. Her coagulation screen (PT, APTT, fibrinogen) is essentially normal. Creatinine is mildly raised.

Structured questions

  1. Part A (8 min). What is your leading diagnosis, and how do you differentiate it from DIC and other thrombotic microangiopathies at the bedside?
  2. Part B (9 min). Outline your immediate management, explicitly justifying what you would and would not transfuse.
  3. Part C (5 min). What confirmatory testing would you send, and how would you monitor response to treatment?
  4. Part D (3 min). Contrast this with how you would manage DIC, and why misdiagnosis between the two is dangerous.
Reveal model answer

Part A — Diagnosis and differentiation

  • This picture — microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (schistocytes, raised LDH, undetectable haptoglobin) with severe thrombocytopenia and a normal coagulation screen — is classic for thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP). The "classic pentad" (fever, MAHA, thrombocytopenia, renal impairment, neurological signs) is not required for diagnosis; most patients present without all five features.
  • Key differentiator from DIC: DIC characteristically shows a deranged coagulation screen (prolonged PT/APTT, low fibrinogen) alongside thrombocytopenia and MAHA, reflecting consumption of clotting factors — this patient's normal coagulation screen argues strongly against DIC.
  • Other differentials (HUS/atypical HUS, sepsis-associated coagulopathy, malignant hypertension, catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome, HELLP if pregnant) should be considered but the normal coagulation profile and clinical picture favour TTP; the PLASMIC score can support pre-test probability while confirmatory testing is pending.

Part B — Immediate management

  • Urgent plasma exchange (PEX) should be started on clinical suspicion — do not wait for ADAMTS13 results, as delay increases mortality; plasma exchange (not simple plasma infusion) is required to both remove the causative ultra-large von Willebrand factor multimers/inhibitory autoantibody and replace ADAMTS13.
  • Avoid platelet transfusion unless there is life-threatening haemorrhage — platelet transfusion can fuel further microvascular thrombosis in TTP and has been associated with clinical deterioration; this is a critical and commonly missed distinction from other thrombocytopenic bleeding scenarios.
  • Adjunctive therapy: corticosteroids, and consider rituximab (particularly for refractory/relapsing disease or to reduce relapse risk) and caplacizumab — the HERCULES trial (Scully et al., NEJM 2019) showed caplacizumab reduced time to platelet count normalisation and the rate of TTP exacerbation and refractory disease, at the cost of increased mucocutaneous bleeding.

Part C — Confirmatory testing and monitoring

  • Send ADAMTS13 activity before starting PEX/plasma if possible (but do not delay treatment for the result) — activity <10% confirms the diagnosis of immune-mediated TTP.
  • Monitor response with daily platelet count and LDH trend; continue PEX daily until platelet count normalises and haemolysis resolves, then taper.
  • Plan follow-up ADAMTS13 monitoring after recovery, as falling activity can precede clinical relapse.

Part D — Contrast with DIC

  • DIC management is fundamentally different: treat the underlying trigger (sepsis, obstetric catastrophe, malignancy), and replace consumed factors with FFP, cryoprecipitate (for fibrinogen), and platelets guided by active bleeding — there is no role for plasma exchange in DIC.
  • Misdiagnosing TTP as DIC risks giving platelet transfusions that worsen thrombosis; misdiagnosing DIC as TTP risks delaying appropriate factor replacement in an actively bleeding patient and pursuing an invasive, resource-intensive therapy (PEX) that will not address the underlying consumptive coagulopathy.

  2. Part B (9 min). How do you structure a family meeting given this disagreement?

Reveal model answer

Part A — Prognostication process

  • Avoid premature prognostication — guidelines recommend waiting at least 72 hours post-arrest (longer if targeted temperature management or sedative/neuromuscular blocking drugs may confound clinical examination) before definitive statements.
  • Use a multimodal approach rather than any single test: clinical examination off sedation (pupillary/corneal reflexes, motor response), EEG (unreactive/burst-suppression patterns are concerning), imaging (diffuse injury on MRI/CT), and biomarkers (e.g. neuron-specific enolase) as adjuncts with their own limitations — no single marker is definitive in isolation.
  • Be alert to and actively guard against a self-fulfilling prophecy, where early pessimistic prognostication itself leads to withdrawal before the true extent of recovery potential can be established.

Part B — Structuring the family meeting

  • Explore the underlying reasons for disagreement — differing values, guilt, gaps in understanding of prognosis, or distrust of the team — rather than simply restating the medical facts more forcefully.
  • Use a structured, unhurried approach: dedicated time, consistent senior clinical messaging across multiple meetings, involvement of a chaplain/psychologist/patient advocate as appropriate, and explicit acknowledgement of the son's distress and hope.
  • Offer an independent second medical opinion and, if disagreement remains intractable, involve the hospital ethics committee/mediation service — this is preferable to unilaterally overriding the family or indefinitely continuing treatment purely to avoid conflict.

Part C — Ethical/legal framework

  • Where continued treatment is judged, after a thorough multidisciplinary and family-inclusive process, not to be in the patient's best interests, the decision to withdraw life-sustaining therapy is typically a clinical best-interests determination made by the treating team — in many jurisdictions this does not legally require unanimous family "consent" in the way a competent patient's own decision would.

Part D — Organ donation

  • Where appropriate, separate the withdrawal-of-treatment conversation from the organ donation conversation where possible, to avoid any perception that donation is influencing the withdrawal decision.
  • Involve the specialist organ donation team early to lead or support this conversation; ensure the family understands they can decline, and that declining does not affect the quality of end-of-life care provided.

CCS_S09 — Tumour Lysis Syndrome: Recognition and Prevention

Short case Renal / Metabolic CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4 20 marks · 5 min read / 12–13 min answer

Opening vignette

A 19-year-old man with newly diagnosed, bulky Burkitt lymphoma starts induction chemotherapy. 18 hours later: potassium 6.2 mmol/L, phosphate 2.8 mmol/L, uric acid 890 µmol/L, corrected calcium 1.6 mmol/L, creatinine rising.

Structured questions

  1. Confirm this diagnosis and comment on his baseline risk category.
  2. What prevention strategy should have been considered before chemotherapy, and why?
  3. Outline your management of the abnormalities in front of you now.
  4. When would you involve nephrology for renal replacement therapy?
Reveal model answer
  • This is tumour lysis syndrome (TLS) per Cairo–Bishop criteria (hyperkalaemia, hyperphosphataemia, hypocalcaemia, hyperuricaemia with rising creatinine). Burkitt lymphoma is a classic high-risk malignancy for TLS given its bulky disease and very high proliferative rate.
  • Prevention in high-risk patients: aggressive IV hydration for all patients, and rasburicase (recombinant urate oxidase) rather than allopurinol — rasburicase rapidly and completely degrades existing uric acid, whereas allopurinol only prevents formation of new uric acid and does not reduce levels already present.   • Management now: continue/escalate rasburicase, manage hyperkalaemia via the standard pathway (see CCS_S08), manage hyperphosphataemia (phosphate binders, dietary restriction, RRT if severe), and only treat hypocalcaemia if symptomatic (tetany, seizures, arrhythmia) — aggressive calcium correction in the presence of hyperphosphataemia risks calcium-phosphate precipitation and worsening renal injury/nephrocalcinosis.
  • Involve nephrology early for RRT if: refractory hyperkalaemia, severe/symptomatic hyperphosphataemia, oliguric AKI, or volume overload — do not wait for creatinine to reach an arbitrary threshold if these features are present.
Marking guide

20 marks: correct diagnosis/risk stratification (5), correct rasburicase-vs-allopurinol reasoning including G6PD screening (6), correct, cautious hypocalcaemia management (5), sensible RRT threshold (4).

Opening vignette

A 48-year-old man is admitted with severe epigastric pain radiating to the back, lipase 5× upper limit of normal, and a rising CRP.   3. What does his rising oxygen requirement after 4 L suggest, and what would you do about it?
  4. What are your other early management priorities?

  • Severity is classified by the revised Atlanta classification primarily on the presence and persistence of organ failure (>48h defines "severe"), rather than by scoring systems alone (APACHE II, BISAP can support risk stratification but should not replace clinical organ-failure assessment).

CCS_S11 — Thyroid Storm: Recognition and Multimodal Treatment

Short case Endocrine / Metabolic-toxic CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4 20 marks · 5 min read / 12–13 min answer

Opening vignette

A 42-year-old woman with known, poorly controlled Graves' disease presents with fever (39.8°C), agitation and delirium, atrial fibrillation with rate 160/min, diarrhoea and profuse sweating, following a recent upper respiratory tract infection.

Structured questions

  1. How do you diagnose thyroid storm, and would you wait for thyroid function tests to treat?
  2. Outline your treatment sequence and the rationale behind the order.
  3. What supportive measures and precipitant management are needed?
  4. What would you do if she remains refractory to initial therapy?
Reveal model answer
  • Thyroid storm is a clinical diagnosis (supported by scoring systems such as the Burch-Wartofsky Point Scale) — treatment should begin immediately on clinical suspicion and must not wait for thyroid function test results.
  • Treatment sequence and rationale: beta-blockade (high-dose propranolol — also inhibits peripheral T4→T3 conversion) started early; a thionamide (propylthiouracil is preferred over carbimazole in storm for its additional peripheral T4→T3 blockade, noting its hepatotoxicity risk); then iodine solution (Lugol's/potassium iodide) given at least one hour after the thionamide — giving iodine before thionamide blockade can transiently fuel new hormone synthesis (Jod-Basedow effect) and worsen the crisis; plus corticosteroids, which reduce peripheral T4→T3 conversion and cover possible relative adrenal insufficiency.
  • Supportive care: active cooling, fluid/electrolyte correction, and treatment of the precipitant (infection here). Use paracetamol, not aspirin, for fever — aspirin displaces thyroid hormone from binding globulin and raises free hormone levels, worsening the crisis.
  • If refractory: consider therapeutic plasma exchange to remove circulating thyroid hormone, and plan definitive therapy (thyroidectomy or radioiodine) once she is stabilised.
Marking guide

20 marks: correct clinical-diagnosis approach without waiting for TFTs (4), correct treatment sequence with rationale, especially iodine timing (8), correct supportive care including aspirin avoidance (4), sensible refractory-disease escalation (4).

Common error / drift trap: Giving iodine before or simultaneously with a thionamide is a classic, dangerous error that can worsen thyroid storm via the Jod-Basedow effect. Iodine must follow thionamide administration by at least an hour. Using aspirin for fever is a second common error given its effect on thyroid hormone binding.

Opening vignette

An 80 kg, 35-year-old man sustains 40% total body surface area burns in a house fire. He has soot in the oropharynx, singed nasal hairs and a hoarse voice, and presents 1 hour post-injury.

Structured questions

  1. What is your immediate airway concern, and what threshold would you use for intubation?

  3. What complications arise from over-resuscitation, and how would you monitor for them?
  4. What other early priorities and toxicological considerations apply here?
Reveal model answer
  • His signs (soot in the oropharynx, singed nasal hairs, hoarse voice) indicate probable inhalational injury and impending airway oedema — intubate early and electively, before stridor or overt airway compromise develops, as oedema progresses over hours and later intubation may be very difficult.

  • Other priorities: analgesia, active warming (large burns cause rapid heat loss), early surgical/burns centre referral for debridement and grafting planning, and screening for carbon monoxide and cyanide toxicity given fire/smoke exposure (carboxyhaemoglobin level; a persistently high lactate in this context raises suspicion for cyanide toxicity, and empirical hydroxocobalamin should be considered if impaired consciousness with a high lactate accompanies significant smoke exposure).

CCS_L11 — Refractory Ventricular Tachycardia Storm

Long case Cardiovascular CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4·5·11 40 marks · 10 min read / 25 min answer

Opening vignette

A 58-year-old man with ischaemic cardiomyopathy (EF 25%) and an ICD presents with recurrent monomorphic VT — 8 ICD shocks in the past hour. He is in VT on the monitor now, BP 70/40 mmHg, GCS 13.

Structured questions

  1. Part A (7 min). Manage the active VT with haemodynamic compromise in front of you.
  2. Part B (8 min). Outline your antiarrhythmic strategy for the ongoing storm.
  3. Part C (7 min). What role does sympathetic blockade play, and what does the evidence show?
  4. Part D (3 min). Outline your plan for definitive therapy and the human impact of recurrent shocks.
Reveal model answer

Part A — Immediate management

  • Synchronised cardioversion for the current haemodynamically compromising VT.
  • Simultaneously correct reversible triggers: electrolytes (potassium, magnesium), assess for ongoing ischaemia (ECG, troponin), review the ICD for lead malposition/inappropriate detection, and review the drug chart for QT-prolonging agents or interactions.

Part B — Antiarrhythmic strategy

  • IV amiodarone loading is first-line for recurrent VT storm; IV lidocaine is a reasonable alternative, particularly for ischaemia-driven VT.
  • Avoid adding further QT-prolonging agents. Consider early deep sedation/general anaesthesia with intubation — this reduces the sympathetic/catecholamine drive that perpetuates the storm and is an important, sometimes under-used, therapeutic lever rather than merely a supportive measure.

Part C — Sympathetic blockade

  • A landmark comparative study (Nademanee et al., Circulation 2000) found that sympathetic blockade (beta-blockade, particularly combined with sedation) was more effective at terminating electrical storm than antiarrhythmic drug therapy alone — this is a key, sometimes underappreciated, principle: VT storm is substantially catecholamine-driven, and blunting that drive is central to management, not just antiarrhythmic drug escalation.
  • For truly refractory cases, adjuncts such as stellate ganglion block or thoracic epidural anaesthesia have supportive case-series evidence for interrupting the storm.
  • VA-ECMO can serve as a haemodynamic bridge in refractory storm, allowing time for further antiarrhythmic titration or definitive ablation without the added burden of maintaining perfusion through recurrent arrhythmia.

Part D — Definitive therapy and human impact

  • Urgent electrophysiology referral for catheter ablation of the arrhythmogenic substrate; address reversible substrate (e.g. revascularisation if ischaemia-driven); review/reprogram the ICD.
  • Recurrent ICD shocks are highly distressing — under deep sedation, consider temporarily deactivating shock therapy (while maintaining monitoring/backup pacing/defibrillation readiness externally) to avoid repeated painful shocks while storm-directed therapy takes effect, then reactivate once controlled.

  2. Part B (7 min). Seizures continue despite an adequate second-line agent. What now?
  3. Part C (7 min). After 24 hours of anaesthetic infusion, seizures recur on any attempt to lighten sedation — she is now in super-refractory status epilepticus. What further options exist?
  4. Part D (4 min). Outline your aetiological work-up and how you would communicate with her family.

Part A — Staged algorithm

  • This is established status epilepticus. Confirm adequate first-line benzodiazepine dosing was given; move promptly to a second-line IV antiepileptic — levetiracetam, valproate, or fosphenytoin/phenytoin.
  • The ESETT trial (Kapur et al., NEJM 2019) found these three second-line agents had similar efficacy and safety (~45–47% seizure cessation across all three) — choice should be individualised by comorbidity/side-effect profile (e.g. avoid valproate if pregnancy or hepatic disease is a concern; caution with phenytoin given arrhythmia/hypotension risk) rather than assuming one agent is superior.

Part B — Refractory status epilepticus

  • Continued seizures after an adequate second-line agent define refractory status epilepticus — proceed to rapid sequence intubation and continuous anaesthetic infusion (midazolam, propofol, or thiopental/pentobarbital), start continuous EEG monitoring, and admit to ICU.

Part C — Super-refractory status epilepticus

  • Options include ketamine infusion (NMDA-receptor mechanism, complementary to GABAergic anaesthetics), higher-dose or combination anaesthesia, and — in selected refractory autoimmune/paraneoplastic presentations — immunotherapy.
  • Given new-onset status epilepticus without a clear precipitant in this previously well patient, actively investigate for autoimmune encephalitis (e.g. anti-NMDA-receptor antibodies) — this is a specific, treatable cause that changes management significantly if found.
  • Other adjuncts with weaker evidence include the ketogenic diet and therapeutic hypothermia; magnesium/pyridoxine are considered if specific relevant risk factors are present (e.g. eclampsia, isoniazid toxicity).

Part D — Aetiological work-up and communication

  • Do not treat status epilepticus symptomatically without pursuing the cause: urgent neuroimaging, LP once safe (infective/autoimmune screen), toxicology, and metabolic screen.
  • Actively manage complications of prolonged status: rhabdomyolysis, hyperthermia, cardiovascular strain, and aspiration risk.
  • Communicate honestly with the family that super-refractory status epilepticus carries significant morbidity and mortality even with maximal therapy, while continuing to actively pursue a treatable cause.

  4. Part D (3 min). What administrative/public health and follow-up considerations apply?

  • WHO severity criteria include: parasitaemia >10%, impaired consciousness (cerebral malaria), AKI, hypoglycaemia, significant acidosis/hyperlactataemia, severe anaemia, pulmonary oedema/ARDS, shock, and spontaneous bleeding/DIC.
  • This patient meets multiple criteria: parasitaemia 12%, confusion (cerebral malaria), AKI (creatinine 220), and hypoglycaemia (2.8 mmol/L) — this is severe, life-threatening malaria requiring ICU-level care.

Part D — Administrative and follow-up considerations

  • Malaria is a notifiable disease in most jurisdictions — notify public health authorities and take a detailed travel/prophylaxis history for surveillance purposes.
  • Screen for co-infections; note that P. falciparum, unlike P. vivax/ovale, has no hypnozoite liver stage and therefore no relapse risk requiring radical-cure therapy (primaquine/ tafenoquine) — a useful differentiator from other malaria species.

  3. What correction limits would you set over the next 24–48 hours, and why?

  • This is an acute exacerbation of IPF (AE-IPF): acute, clinically significant respiratory deterioration (typically <1 month) with new bilateral ground-glass/consolidation superimposed on a UIP pattern, not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload.
  • AE-IPF is partly a diagnosis of exclusion — actively rule out infection, pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, drug toxicity (e.g. amiodarone), and aspiration, as these are treatable alternative or contributing causes.

  • Unlike typical ARDS, the fibrotic IPF lung has essentially no capacity for meaningful recovery — invasive mechanical ventilation for AE-IPF, outside a bridge-to-transplant context, has historically been associated with very poor (often near-universal) short-term mortality.

  • Because he is already listed for transplant, ECMO as a bridge-to-transplant is an increasingly established option — in contrast to general reluctance to offer ECMO in AE-IPF without transplant candidacy, given the native lung's poor recovery potential.

Opening vignette

A 58-year-old woman has an acute right MCA territory ischaemic stroke (NIHSS 22), presenting outside the thrombolysis/thrombectomy window. By day 2, GCS has fallen from 14 to 10; repeat CT shows an established large MCA infarct with 6 mm midline shift.

Structured questions

  1. Part A (6 min). Why is she at high risk of malignant MCA syndrome?
  2. Part B (7 min). Outline your medical management of raised ICP here.
  3. Part C (9 min). Discuss the evidence for decompressive hemicraniectomy, including any important caveats.
  4. Part D (3 min). How do you frame this decision with the patient's family?
Reveal model answer

Part A — Malignant MCA risk

  • Malignant MCA syndrome is predicted by large infarct volume, high NIHSS (>15–20), and rapid early clinical decline — all present here, with falling GCS and radiographic midline shift confirming a malignant trajectory driven by cytotoxic oedema.

Part B — Medical management

  • Similar principles to raised ICP management generally (head-up positioning, avoid hypercapnia/ hypoxia, osmotherapy), but note the oedema in malignant MCA infarction typically peaks days 2–5 (a different timeline to TBI) — and medical therapy alone carries a poor outcome for malignant MCA infarction specifically, given the scale and progression of oedema.

Part C — Decompressive hemicraniectomy evidence

  • A pooled analysis of the DECIMAL, DESTINY, and HAMLET trials (Vahedi et al., Lancet Neurol 2007) showed decompressive hemicraniectomy within 48 hours significantly reduced mortality and increased the proportion of patients with a favourable outcome (mRS ≤4).
  • This benefit came with more survivors in a state of moderate-to-severe disability (mRS 3–4) rather than good functional recovery — the same trade-off seen with decompressive craniectomy in TBI.
  • Age matters: DESTINY II (Jüttler et al., NEJM 2014), extending evaluation to patients over 60, confirmed a survival benefit but with a less favourable disability profile in this older group — raising important quality-of-life and patient-values considerations that should be explicit in decision-making, particularly for older patients.

CCS_S26 — Adrenal Crisis

Short case Endocrine / Metabolic-toxic CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4 20 marks · 5 min read / 12–13 min answer

Opening vignette

A 48-year-old woman with known Addison's disease presents with vomiting and diarrhoea, profound hypotension unresponsive to fluid boluses, hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, and hypoglycaemia.

Structured questions

  1. What is your diagnosis, and would you wait for a cortisol level to treat?
  2. Outline your immediate treatment.
  3. How do you manage the fluid and electrolyte abnormalities?
  4. What longer-term measures would prevent recurrence?
Reveal model answer
  • This is an adrenal crisis precipitated by intercurrent gastroenteritis (likely failure to stress-dose steroids), with the classic biochemistry of hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, hypoglycaemia, and fluid-refractory hypotension.
  • Treatment must not wait for a cortisol result — give IV hydrocortisone 100 mg immediately on clinical suspicion (sending cortisol/ACTH before or alongside the first dose if possible), followed by ongoing hydrocortisone (continuous infusion or 6-hourly dosing).
  • Aggressive IV 0.9% saline resuscitation — this also helps correct the hyponatraemia; correct hypoglycaemia with IV dextrose. Hyperkalaemia usually improves with hydrocortisone and fluid without needing specific potassium-lowering therapy unless severe or symptomatic.
  • Identify and treat the precipitant (infection here); on recovery, ensure "sick day rules" education (stress dosing during intercurrent illness), a steroid emergency card/alert bracelet, and endocrinology follow-up.
Marking guide

20 marks: correct diagnosis and immediate, no-delay treatment principle (6), correct hydrocortisone dosing/regimen (5), correct fluid/electrolyte management (5), sensible secondary prevention (4).

Common error / drift trap: Waiting for a cortisol level before treating suspected adrenal crisis is a dangerous, classic error. Treatment should be given immediately on clinical suspicion, never delayed for a laboratory result, given the risk of rapid, potentially fatal deterioration.

CCS_S27 — Severe Pre-eclampsia and Eclampsia

Short case Obstetric / Peri-partum CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4 20 marks · 5 min read / 12–13 min answer

Opening vignette

A woman at 32 weeks' gestation has BP 175/115 mmHg, proteinuria, headache, and hyperreflexia, then has a witnessed generalised tonic-clonic seizure.

Structured questions

  1. What is your immediate seizure management?
  2. What is your first-line agent for treating and preventing further seizures, and what is the evidence?
  3. How would you approach blood pressure control?
  4. What is the definitive treatment, and how do you plan it?
Reveal model answer
  • Immediate seizure management: standard ABC approach, left lateral position, protect the airway.
  • Magnesium sulfate is first-line for both treatment and prevention of further eclamptic seizures — the Collaborative Eclampsia Trial (Lancet 1995) showed it superior to both diazepam and phenytoin, and the MAGPIE trial (Lancet 2002) confirmed its role in prevention. Give a loading dose then maintenance infusion, monitoring for toxicity (reduced reflexes, respiratory depression, cardiac effects — particularly with renal impairment), with calcium gluconate available as the antidote.
  • BP control with agents such as labetalol, hydralazine, or nifedipine per local protocol — avoid ACE inhibitors/ARBs (teratogenic/fetotoxic), and avoid overly aggressive reduction given placental perfusion concerns.
  • The definitive treatment is delivery — timing depends on gestational age and maternal/fetal stability, with full MDT involvement (obstetrics, anaesthesia, neonatology); give steroids for fetal lung maturity if preterm and time allows, and continue magnesium for 24 hours post-delivery/last seizure for ongoing prophylaxis.
Marking guide

20 marks: correct immediate seizure management (4), correct magnesium-first-line evidence (6), correct BP agents and cautions (5), correct delivery-focused definitive plan (5).

Guideline-drift trap: Defaulting to standard anticonvulsants (phenytoin, benzodiazepines) for eclamptic seizures is incorrect. Magnesium sulfate is specifically superior to both for treatment and prevention of eclamptic seizures and should be first-line, not a standard antiepileptic drug.

CCS_S28 — Amniotic Fluid Embolism

Short case Obstetric / Peri-partum CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4·5 20 marks · 5 min read / 12–13 min answer

Opening vignette

A woman in active labour suddenly collapses with hypoxia and hypotension, progressing to cardiac arrest. After return of spontaneous circulation, she develops profuse bleeding with evidence of DIC.

Structured questions

  1. What is your leading diagnosis, and what else must you consider?
  2. Outline your immediate resuscitation, including a critical time-sensitive intervention.
  3. How would you manage the coagulopathy?
  4. What advanced/supportive therapy might you consider for refractory collapse?
Reveal model answer
  • Amniotic fluid embolism (AFE) is a clinical diagnosis of exclusion — the classic triad of sudden hypoxia, cardiovascular collapse, and coagulopathy/DIC occurring during labour/delivery or shortly after. Differentials include pulmonary embolism, eclampsia, anaphylaxis, local anaesthetic toxicity, and haemorrhage.
  • Standard maternal cardiac arrest algorithm: left uterine displacement/tilt, high-quality CPR, and perimortem caesarean section within 4–5 minutes if no ROSC in a viable pregnancy — this improves maternal resuscitation (relieving aortocaval compression) as well as fetal outcome, and should not be delayed while further resuscitation attempts are made.
  • AFE causes severe, early DIC — manage with aggressive factor replacement (FFP, cryoprecipitate/ fibrinogen, platelets), ideally guided by viscoelastic testing, using the same massive transfusion principles as major obstetric haemorrhage.
  • Consider VA-ECMO for refractory cardiovascular collapse as a rescue option; ensure structured MDT debrief and psychological support given the catastrophic, often fatal, nature of AFE.
Marking guide

20 marks: correct diagnosis and differential (5), correct, time-critical perimortem caesarean timing (6), appropriate DIC/coagulopathy management (5), sensible advanced-therapy/debrief considerations (4).

Common error / drift trap: Delaying perimortem caesarean section to continue further resuscitation attempts is a common error. Current guidance is to perform it within 4–5 minutes of maternal cardiac arrest without ROSC in a viable pregnancy, as delay reduces the chance of a good outcome for both mother and baby.

CCS_S29 — Traumatic Spinal Cord Injury: Neurogenic Shock and Steroid Controversy

Short case Trauma / Burns / Environmental CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·4 20 marks · 5 min read / 12–13 min answer

Opening vignette

A 22-year-old man has a C5 fracture-dislocation with cord injury and flaccid paralysis below C5 following a diving accident. BP 80/50 mmHg, HR 45/min, with warm peripheries.

Structured questions

  1. What is causing his haemodynamic picture, and how does it differ from haemorrhagic shock?
  2. What are your haemodynamic targets and management?
  3. Would you give high-dose methylprednisolone? Justify your answer.
  4. What other early priorities apply?
Reveal model answer
  • This is neurogenic shock — distributive shock from loss of sympathetic tone below the level of injury, producing hypotension with bradycardia and warm peripheries, distinct from haemorrhagic/hypovolaemic shock (which would show tachycardia and cool peripheries). (Note: "spinal shock" is a separate concept — transient loss of reflexes/motor function below the injury level — not the same as the haemodynamic phenomenon of neurogenic shock.)
  • Target MAP around 85–90 mmHg for the first 5–7 days to optimise spinal cord perfusion pressure (based on observational evidence/consensus guidance rather than high-quality RCTs); use vasopressors (norepinephrine, given the need for both vasoconstriction and some inotropy) rather than relying on fluids alone, with atropine/pacing for symptomatic bradycardia, and careful fluid balance given the risk of pulmonary oedema with over-resuscitation.
  • Do not give high-dose methylprednisolone. Although NASCIS II/III historically suggested a possible modest benefit if given within 8 hours of injury, subsequent re-analysis and current major guidelines (AANS/CNS) conclude the evidence is weak/flawed and that the risk of complications (infection, GI bleeding, hyperglycaemia) outweighs any marginal, uncertain benefit — routine high-dose steroids are not recommended for acute traumatic spinal cord injury.
  • Other priorities: urgent spinal imaging and surgical decompression assessment (earlier decompression, <24h, is associated with improved neurological outcomes in some studies), DVT prophylaxis (high risk), meticulous pressure area care, bladder/bowel management planning, and early rehabilitation involvement.
Marking guide

20 marks: correct neurogenic-shock physiology and distinction from haemorrhagic shock (5), correct MAP target/vasopressor strategy (5), correct, evidence-based refusal of high-dose steroids (6 — see drift trap), sensible additional priorities (4).

Guideline-drift trap: High-dose methylprednisolone for acute spinal cord injury is a classic example of guideline reversal — once standard teaching based on NASCIS II/III, current major guidelines do not recommend routine use given weak evidence and significant complication risk. Candidates recommending it as standard therapy without acknowledging this reversal should lose marks.

  1. Fehlings MG, Tetreault LA, Wilson JR, et al. A Clinical Practice Guideline for the Management of Patients With Acute Spinal Cord Injury: Recommendations on the Role of Pharmacological Therapy. Global Spine J. 2017;7(3 Suppl):203S–211S.
  2. Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine. Early Acute Management in Adults with Spinal Cord Injury: A Clinical Practice Guideline for Health-Care Professionals.

CCS_S30 — Determination of Death by Neurological Criteria

Short case End-of-Life / Ethics / Communication CoBaTrICE 1·2·3·8·11·12 20 marks · 5 min read / 12–13 min answer

Opening vignette

A 40-year-old man with a devastating subarachnoid haemorrhage has GCS 3 with fixed, dilated pupils. He has been off sedation for more than 24 hours with no identified confounders. The team is considering testing for death by neurological criteria.

Structured questions

  1. What preconditions must be met before testing?
  2. Outline the clinical testing components.
  3. When, if ever, are ancillary tests required?
  4. How do you document the time of death, and how do you explain this to the family?
Reveal model answer
  • Preconditions: an irreversible catastrophic brain injury of known cause, with exclusion of confounders — residual sedative/neuromuscular blocking drug effect (check levels/timing appropriate to organ function), resolved effects of any therapeutic hypothermia, severe metabolic/endocrine derangement, hypotension, and hypothermia below the locally defined threshold — all must be corrected before testing.
  • Clinical testing: absence of all brainstem reflexes (pupillary, corneal, oculovestibular/caloric, gag/cough) and a formal apnoea test (pre-oxygenation, monitoring for an adequate rise in PaCO2 to confirm no respiratory drive, aborting if haemodynamically unstable or desaturating). Testing must be performed by two appropriately qualified/experienced clinicians (per local protocol — e.g. in the UK, two doctors, one a consultant, testing together, on two occasions).
  • Ancillary tests are not routinely required in most major jurisdictions (including the UK) when full, unconfounded clinical testing is completed and unequivocal — they are reserved for situations where clinical testing cannot be completed or confounders cannot be excluded (e.g. facial trauma precluding cranial nerve testing, inability to safely perform the apnoea test).
  • The time of death is the time the confirmatory test is completed, not when organ support is subsequently withdrawn. Communicate to the family clearly that death has already occurred, distinguishing this from the ongoing appearance of a beating heart on a ventilator — a common and important source of family confusion requiring careful, explicit explanation.
Marking guide

20 marks: correct preconditions/exclusions (6), correct clinical testing components including apnoea test safety (6), correct statement that ancillary testing is not routinely required (4), correct time-of- death documentation and sensitive family explanation (4).

Common error / drift trap: Many candidates assume imaging or EEG confirmation is always required for brain death determination. In most major jurisdictions, thorough, unconfounded clinical testing by two appropriately qualified doctors is sufficient, and ancillary tests are reserved for cases where clinical testing cannot be reliably completed — not a routine requirement.
