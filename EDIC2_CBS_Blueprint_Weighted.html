How to use this resource

This bank contains 200 EDIC Part II–style stations:

  • 50 CCS (20 long, 30 short)
  • 150 CBS (50 imaging, 50 curves/waveforms, 50 biochemistry/data)

All stations are consultant-level, aligned with the EDIC Guidelines 2023–2026 and the CoBaTrICE syllabus.

Suggested approach

CCS stations

  • Long cases: allow yourself 10 minutes to read the opening vignette, then 25 minutes to answer out loud in structured bullet points, as in the real exam.
  • Short cases: 5 minutes reading, 12–13 minutes answering.
  • Answer out loud first, then write your bullet points. If you cannot say it in structured order in 60 seconds, you do not truly have it.
  • Before revealing the model answer, set your confidence: Confident / Unsure / Guessing. Do not adjust it afterwards.
  • Then reveal the model answer and mark yourself as an examiner would. Partial credit is allowed; benefit of the doubt is not.

CBS stations

  • Treat each as a 12-minute station with 8–12 expected answers.
  • Go through the images/curves/data in order, speaking your answers out loud, then write them down.
  • Set confidence before revealing, then mark yourself strictly.

Tracking your progress

  • For each station, record: confidence, marks awarded, and total marks.
  • Periodically review your performance by domain (respiratory, cardiovascular, neuro, etc.) to identify weak areas.
  • Re-sit selected stations after 2–4 weeks without re-reading the model answers first; the delta is the only measure that matters.

Blueprint coverage

  • The distribution of stations follows the EDIC Part II blueprint derived from CoBaTrICE:
    • Heavy emphasis on respiratory, cardiovascular, neurology, and infection.
    • Substantial coverage of renal/metabolic, GI/hepatic, haematology/oncology, endocrine/metabolic-toxic, obstetric, trauma, and end-of-life/ethics.
  • CBS stations emphasise data interpretation (imaging, curves, biochemistry) across all systems.

Use this bank as a calibration and gap-diagnostic tool, not just a question list. The goal is to expose guideline drift, strengthen structured verbal answers, and build exam-ready fluency.

CBS_IMG_01 — Portable Chest Radiograph in Severe Hypoxaemic Respiratory Failure

Imaging Respiratory CoBaTrICE 2·3·4 10 marks · 12 min

Clinical context

A 61-year-old woman, ventilated for 36 hours for severe community-acquired pneumonia, has a supine portable AP chest radiograph performed for worsening oxygenation (PaO2/FiO2 95 mmHg). Endotracheal tube and a right internal jugular central line are in place.

Search links only (direct/topic-page links were dropped — less reliable and not verifiable from this session). Individual images on these sites are typically CC BY-NC-SA — check the licence on the specific case before reusing or redistributing it yourself.

Structured questions

  1. Describe this radiograph systematically, as you would present it to an examiner.
  2. What is your differential diagnosis for this pattern, and which single bedside investigation would most efficiently narrow it in this patient?
  3. Relate the appearance to a formal diagnostic framework for this patient's respiratory failure.
  4. Name three further tests or manoeuvres you would perform in the next hour and what each would add.
Reveal model answer

1. Systematic description

  • Confirm patient details, projection (AP, supine, portable), rotation and inspiratory effort — adequacy should be caveated for a portable supine film.
  • Lines/tubes: endotracheal tube tip appropriately positioned above the carina (roughly 3–5 cm above, mid-trachea with neck neutral); right internal jugular central line with tip projecting appropriately at the cavoatrial junction — state you would confirm on a magnified view.
  • Lung fields: bilateral, fairly symmetric, diffuse airspace (alveolar) opacification with visible air bronchograms, sparing no zone — consistent with diffuse alveolar filling rather than a focal process.
  • Heart size: cannot be reliably assessed on a supine AP film (cardiothoracic ratio not valid), but no gross cardiomegaly apparent; no obvious upper-lobe diversion or peribronchial cuffing to strongly favour cardiogenic oedema, though supine positioning limits this assessment.
  • Pleura: no obvious pneumothorax or large effusion; costophrenic angles not fully assessable supine.
  • Soft tissues/bones: no surgical emphysema, no obvious fracture.

2. Differential diagnosis and bedside investigation

  • Differential: ARDS (from pneumonia/sepsis as here), cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, diffuse alveolar haemorrhage, aspiration, acute interstitial pneumonia/acute eosinophilic pneumonia, less likely miliary infection given acuity.
  • The single most efficient bedside test to narrow this is focused critical care echocardiography — assessing LV systolic function, filling status, and excluding significant cardiogenic contribution, which is central to the Berlin Definition's requirement to exclude cardiac failure as the sole/main explanation.

3. Formal diagnostic framework

  • Apply the Berlin Definition of ARDS: acute onset (within 1 week of a known insult — here, pneumonia), bilateral opacities not fully explained by effusion/collapse/nodules, and respiratory failure not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload (requires objective assessment, e.g. echo, if no clear ARDS risk factor — though a clear risk factor is present here).
  • With PaO2/FiO2 95 mmHg on adequate PEEP, this represents moderate ARDS (101–200 mmHg band; note 95 mmHg would be at the moderate/severe boundary — confirm the PEEP level at which this was measured, as the ratio is only valid at PEEP/CPAP ≥5 cmH2O).

4. Further tests/manoeuvres in the next hour

  • Focused echocardiography — as above, to exclude a significant cardiogenic contribution and assess RV function/strain from high intrathoracic pressures.
  • Repeat/confirm ETT and line position on a magnified view or by direct laryngoscopy if any doubt, given the implications of a malpositioned airway device in a hypoxaemic patient.
  • Lung ultrasound at the bedside — rapid, radiation-free assessment for B-lines (interstitial/alveolar syndrome), effusion, and to help exclude pneumothorax; complements the radiograph and can be repeated serially without transport risk.
Marking guide

Award up to 10 marks across: systematic ABCDE-style description (3), correct identification of ETT/line position as first priority (1), a coherent differential diagnosis (2), correct application of the Berlin Definition with correct severity band (2), sensible next steps with justification (2).

Common error / drift trap: Candidates frequently attempt to comment on cardiothoracic ratio or use classic upright-film cardiomegaly criteria on a supine AP portable film — this is invalid and should be explicitly flagged as a limitation rather than quantified. Similarly, candidates should not state the PaO2/FiO2 ratio is diagnostic in isolation without confirming it was measured on PEEP/CPAP ≥5 cmH2O.

Imaging Cardiovascular CoBaTrICE 2·3·4 10 marks · 12 min

Clinical context

A 55-year-old woman collapses with sudden hypotension (BP 82/54 mmHg) and hypoxaemia. CT pulmonary angiogram is performed.

Fig. 1 — Original schematic diagram constructed to represent the described CTPA findings for teaching purposes.

Real reference images (external, open-license)

  • Radiopaedia — search: "saddle pulmonary embolus CTPA" (cases)

Search link only — not verified from within this session.

Structured questions

  1. Describe the filling defect and its location.
  2. What additional measurement on this same study is essential to risk-stratify her, and why?
  3. What other CT signs would you actively look for?
  4. How does this correlate with her clinical risk category and management?
Reveal model answer
  • A large filling defect straddles the pulmonary artery bifurcation and extends into both main pulmonary arteries — a saddle embolus.
  • Essential additional assessment: the RV:LV diameter ratio on the same study — a ratio >1.0 indicates right ventricular strain and is associated with worse prognosis, independent of the clot burden itself.
  • Other signs of RV strain to seek: interventricular septal bowing/flattening toward the LV, reflux of contrast into the IVC/hepatic veins, and a dilated main pulmonary artery.
  • Given her hypotension (high-risk/"massive" PE by ESC classification), the priority is immediate reperfusion consideration rather than anticoagulation alone — see CCS_S05 for the full management discussion, including the tension around thrombolysis in specific bleeding-risk contexts.
Marking guide

10 marks: correct identification/location of the filling defect (3), correct emphasis on RV:LV ratio as essential (4), other strain signs named (2), correct link to clinical risk category (1).

Common error / drift trap: Focusing only on identifying the clot without assessing RV strain is a common omission — the RV:LV ratio and secondary strain signs are as important as the embolus itself for risk-stratifying and guiding therapy.

References

  1. Konstantinides SV, Meyer G, Becattini C, et al. 2019 ESC Guidelines for the diagnosis and management of acute pulmonary embolism. Eur Heart J. 2020;41(4):543–603.

CBS_IMG_03 — Aortic Dissection on CT Aortogram

Imaging Cardiovascular CoBaTrICE 2·3·4 10 marks · 12 min

Clinical context

A 60-year-old hypertensive man presents with sudden tearing chest pain radiating to the back. CT aortogram is performed.

Fig. 1 — Original schematic diagram constructed to represent the described CT aortogram findings for teaching purposes.

Real reference images (external, open-license)

  • Radiopaedia — search: "aortic dissection CT angiogram" (cases)

Search link only — not verified from within this session.

Structured questions

  1. Describe the key finding and how you distinguish true from false lumen.
  2. Classify this dissection and explain why the classification matters.
  3. What complications would you actively look for on this study?
  4. How does management differ by classification?
Reveal model answer
  • An intimal flap divides the aortic lumen into a true and false lumen. The true lumen is typically smaller, higher-attenuation, and continuous with the undissected aorta; the false lumen is often larger and crescentic, sometimes showing a "cobweb" sign or slower flow.
  • Stanford classification: Type A involves the ascending aorta (surgical emergency); Type B is confined to the descending aorta distal to the left subclavian artery (generally managed medically unless complicated). This distinction fundamentally changes management.
  • Actively look for: pericardial effusion/tamponade, malperfusion of branch vessels (coronary, renal, mesenteric, limb), and aortic regurgitation.
  • Management: Type A is a surgical emergency requiring urgent cardiothoracic surgery. Uncomplicated Type B is managed medically with aggressive heart rate/blood pressure control ("impulse control" therapy); complicated Type B (malperfusion, rupture, refractory pain/hypertension) requires intervention, typically TEVAR.
Marking guide

10 marks: correct true/false lumen distinction (3), correct Stanford classification and rationale (3), complications named (2), correct management distinction by type (2).

Common error / drift trap: Assuming all aortic dissections require emergency surgery is incorrect — uncomplicated Type B dissections are generally managed medically; conversely, a Type A dissection is a surgical emergency and should never be managed medically alone.

References

  1. Erbel R, Aboyans V, Boileau C, et al. 2014 ESC Guidelines on the diagnosis and treatment of aortic diseases. Eur Heart J. 2014;35(41):2873–2926.

A ventilated ICU patient desaturates suddenly. A supine portable chest radiograph is obtained (an erect film is not possible).

Fig. 1 — Original schematic diagram constructed to represent the described radiographic pattern for teaching purposes.

Real reference images (external, open-license)

  • Radiopaedia — search: "deep sulcus sign pneumothorax" (cases)

Search link only — not verified from within this session.

Structured questions

  1. Why are supine films insensitive for pneumothorax, and what subtle sign is shown here?
  2. What other supine-specific signs would you look for?
  3. If this patient had tension physiology, would you wait for this film before treating?
  4. How would you confirm the diagnosis if it remained uncertain and the patient was stable?
Reveal model answer
  • Supine films are insensitive for pneumothorax because free air rises anteriorly and medially rather than apically as on an erect film, so the classic apical lucent rim with a visible visceral pleural line may not be seen. The key subtle sign here is the deep sulcus sign — an abnormally deep, lucent costophrenic angle.
  • Other supine signs: a hyperlucent upper quadrant, an unusually sharply outlined cardiac border/ mediastinum, a visible anterior costophrenic sulcus, and the "double diaphragm" sign.
  • No — with tension physiology, treat immediately on clinical grounds without waiting for imaging (see CCS_S13); imaging should never delay decompression in a haemodynamically compromising tension pneumothorax.
  • If stable and diagnosis uncertain, bedside lung ultrasound is more sensitive than a supine film — look for absent lung sliding and the "barcode"/"stratosphere" sign on M-mode.
Marking guide

10 marks: correct explanation of supine insensitivity and deep sulcus sign (4), other supine signs named (2), correct "treat, don't wait for imaging" answer for tension physiology (2), correct ultrasound confirmation answer (2).

Common error / drift trap: Relying on classic erect-film pneumothorax signs on a supine ICU film can miss the diagnosis — the deep sulcus sign and other subtle supine-specific signs must be actively sought, and bedside ultrasound is more sensitive than a supine film in this setting.

Imaging Respiratory CoBaTrICE 2·3·4 10 marks · 12 min

Clinical context

Two ICU patients each have a dense, near-complete "white-out" of the left hemithorax on chest radiograph. Compare the two.

Fig. 1 — Original schematic diagrams constructed to represent the described radiographic patterns for teaching purposes.

Real reference images (external, open-license)

  • Radiopaedia — search: "massive pleural effusion vs lung collapse chest x-ray" (cases)

Search link only — not verified from within this session.

Structured questions

  1. State the single most useful discriminating sign between the two panels, and apply it.
  2. What other features support each diagnosis?
  3. How would you confirm and further characterise each?
  4. How does management differ between the two?
Reveal model answer
  • The key discriminator is the direction of mediastinal shift: a large space-occupying process (effusion) pushes the mediastinum away from the white-out (Panel A); volume loss (collapse) pulls the mediastinum toward the white-out (Panel B).
  • Supporting features — effusion: a meniscus sign, relatively uniform density, costophrenic angle blunting. Collapse: volume-loss signs such as crowded ribs, an elevated hemidiaphragm, and displaced fissures.
  • Confirm with bedside ultrasound (rapid, distinguishes fluid from consolidation/collapse, guides thoracocentesis) or CT if diagnosis remains unclear.
  • Management differs fundamentally: a large symptomatic effusion requires drainage; collapse requires identifying and treating the cause (e.g. mucus plug — consider bronchoscopy, physiotherapy).
Marking guide

10 marks: correct identification of shift direction as the key sign, applied correctly to both panels (5), supporting features named (2), sensible confirmatory imaging (1), correct, contrasting management (2).

Common error / drift trap: Not actively assessing mediastinal shift direction is a common oversight that can lead to misdiagnosis between a large effusion and lobar/lung collapse — two conditions with opposite management implications (drainage vs bronchoscopy/physiotherapy).

Imaging Neurology / Neurocritical Care CoBaTrICE 2·3·4 10 marks · 12 min

A 45-year-old woman presents with sudden "thunderclap" headache. Non-contrast CT head is performed within 3 hours of onset.

Fig. 1 — Original schematic diagram constructed to represent the described CT findings for teaching purposes.

Real reference images (external, open-license)

  • Radiopaedia — search: "subarachnoid haemorrhage non-contrast CT" (cases)

Search link only — not verified from within this session.

Structured questions

  1. Describe the pattern shown and state the diagnosis.
  2. How would you grade this using a validated scale, and why does grading matter?
  3. How does CT sensitivity change with time from symptom onset, and what does this mean for a negative scan?
  4. What is your next investigation?
Reveal model answer
  • Star-shaped hyperdensity filling the basal cisterns and proximal sulci is the classic appearance of subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH).
  • Grade using the modified Fisher scale, which correlates blood quantity/distribution with the risk of subsequent vasospasm/delayed cerebral ischaemia — a thick, diffuse pattern like this one places her at higher risk, informing surveillance intensity (see CCS_S06).
  • CT sensitivity for SAH is very high (~95–98%) within the first 6 hours of onset, but declines substantially over the following days as blood is resorbed. A negative CT beyond this window, especially with a convincing thunderclap headache history, does not exclude SAH and warrants further investigation.
  • Next step: CT angiography to identify a causative aneurysm and guide urgent neurosurgical/ interventional referral.
Marking guide

10 marks: correct pattern description and diagnosis (3), correct grading scale and its purpose (3), correct time-dependent sensitivity point (2), correct next investigation (2).

Common error / drift trap: A negative CT is sometimes assumed to definitively exclude SAH regardless of timing. Sensitivity is very high within the first 6 hours but declines significantly thereafter — a negative scan beyond this window with a convincing history still warrants further investigation (LP for xanthochromia or CT angiography), not reassurance alone.

CBS_IMG_07 — Extradural vs Subdural Haematoma

Imaging Neurology / Neurocritical Care CoBaTrICE 2·3·4 10 marks · 12 min

Clinical context

Two trauma patients each have an extra-axial haematoma on CT head. Compare their appearances.

Fig. 1 — Original schematic diagrams constructed to represent the described CT findings for teaching purposes.

Real reference images (external, open-license)

  • Radiopaedia — search: "extradural vs subdural haematoma CT" (cases)

Search link only — not verified from within this session.

Structured questions

  1. Describe the shape difference between the two and explain why it occurs.
  2. What is the typical mechanism/vessel for each?
  3. How does appearance change with age of the haematoma, and what pitfall does this create?
  4. What determines the threshold for surgical evacuation?
Reveal model answer
  • Extradural haematoma (EDH): biconvex/lentiform, does not cross suture lines — limited by dural attachment at the sutures. Subdural haematoma (SDH): crescentic, crosses suture lines but is limited by dural reflections (falx, tentorium).
  • EDH classically arises from middle meningeal artery injury, often with a "lucid interval" following initial loss of consciousness. SDH arises from bridging vein rupture, more common in elderly or anticoagulated patients or those with brain atrophy (more vein stretch/vulnerability).
  • Acute blood is hyperdense on CT; a subacute SDH becomes isodense to brain parenchyma over roughly 1–3 weeks and is easily missed if not specifically sought (subtle signs: sulcal effacement, midline shift without an obviously visible hyperdense collection, ventricular asymmetry); chronic SDH becomes hypodense.
  • Surgical evacuation thresholds depend on size/midline shift, GCS trajectory, and local neurosurgical guidance (e.g. Brain Trauma Foundation-informed criteria) — small, asymptomatic collections may be managed conservatively with serial imaging.
Marking guide

10 marks: correct shape distinction and mechanism (suture-limited vs falx-limited) (4), correct vessel/mechanism for each (2), correct subacute-isodense pitfall (2), sensible surgical threshold reasoning (2).

Common error / drift trap: Subacute SDH can become isodense to brain (roughly 1–3 weeks post-injury) and is easily missed if not specifically looked for — a "normal-looking" scan in a patient with a compatible history and appropriate timing should prompt closer scrutiny or MRI if suspicion remains.

CBS_IMG_08 — Pneumoperitoneum on Erect Chest Radiograph

Imaging GI / Hepatic / Pancreas CoBaTrICE 2·3·4 10 marks · 12 min

Clinical context

A 70-year-old man presents with sudden severe abdominal pain and peritonism. An erect chest radiograph is performed.

Fig. 1 — Original schematic diagram constructed to represent the described radiographic finding for teaching purposes.

Real reference images (external, open-license)

  • Radiopaedia — search: "pneumoperitoneum erect chest x-ray" (cases)

Search link only — not verified from within this session.

Structured questions

  1. Describe the finding and name it.
  2. What alternative or adjunct radiographic signs exist if an erect film is not possible?
  3. Is free air always due to perforation? What must you consider?
  4. What is your immediate management?
Reveal model answer
  • A crescent of lucent free gas beneath the right hemidiaphragm, above the liver — pneumoperitoneum, indicating a perforated viscus until proven otherwise.
  • If an erect film cannot be obtained: a lateral decubitus film (air rises to the non-dependent side), Rigler's sign on a supine film (air outlining both sides of the bowel wall), or the "football sign" (large-volume free air outlining the entire peritoneal cavity).
  • Free air is not always due to perforation — recent abdominal surgery (open or laparoscopic) can produce visible free air for up to 7–10 days postoperatively as a normal finding. Clinical correlation (new peritonism, timing relative to any recent surgery) is essential.
  • In a clinically clear picture of perforation with peritonism, this is a surgical emergency — proceed to urgent laparotomy after appropriate resuscitation, without delaying for further imaging.
Marking guide

10 marks: correct identification/naming of the finding (3), alternative signs named (2), correct recognition that recent surgery is a normal-finding pitfall (3), correct urgent management (2).

Common error / drift trap: Free intraperitoneal air is not always due to visceral perforation — recent abdominal surgery can produce visible free air for up to 7–10 days postoperatively as a normal finding; clinical correlation is essential before assuming perforation in a post-surgical patient.

CBS_IMG_10 — Traumatic Aortic Injury: Widened Mediastinum

Imaging Trauma / Burns / Environmental CoBaTrICE 2·3·4 10 marks · 12 min

Clinical context

A 30-year-old man sustains a high-speed deceleration motor vehicle collision. A supine trauma chest radiograph is obtained.

Fig. 1 — Original schematic diagram constructed to represent the described radiographic findings for teaching purposes.

Real reference images (external, open-license)

  • Radiopaedia — search: "traumatic aortic injury widened mediastinum" (cases)

Search link only — not verified from within this session.

Structured questions

  1. List the signs shown and their significance.
  2. How sensitive/specific is this film for the underlying injury?
  3. What is the typical mechanism and anatomical site of injury?
  4. Outline your management approach, including blood pressure control.
Reveal model answer
  • Signs of possible traumatic aortic injury: widened mediastinum (>8 cm on a supine AP film), loss of the normal aortic knob contour, a left apical pleural cap, and tracheal deviation to the right — all raise concern for great vessel injury.
  • This chest radiograph is a screening tool only — insensitive and non-specific (many false positives from rotation/technique, and it can miss injury). CT aortography is the definitive diagnostic test and should follow whenever suspicion exists, regardless of the plain film appearance.
  • Mechanism: rapid deceleration. Injury typically occurs at the aortic isthmus, just distal to the left subclavian artery origin, where the relatively mobile aortic arch meets the fixed descending aorta tethered by the ligamentum arteriosum.
  • Management: grade of injury determines urgency — free rupture is immediately life-threatening, while contained/minor injury may allow permissive hypotension and delayed repair if other injuries take immediate priority. TEVAR is now generally preferred over open repair. Regardless of repair timing, blood pressure/heart rate control (e.g. beta-blockade) to reduce aortic wall stress is important while awaiting definitive management.
Marking guide

10 marks: correct signs and significance (3), correct sensitivity/specificity limitation and need for CT aortography regardless of CXR appearance (3), correct mechanism/anatomical site (2), correct management including BP/HR control (2).

Common error / drift trap: A normal-appearing supine trauma chest X-ray is sometimes taken as reassurance against traumatic aortic injury. CXR is only a screening tool with limited sensitivity/specificity for this injury — CT aortography is required for definitive exclusion whenever the mechanism is compatible, regardless of CXR appearance.

  2. What is the underlying pathophysiology, and how does it explain the fall in blood pressure?
  3. What is your immediate action given the hypotension, before any ventilator adjustment?
  4. Outline your subsequent ventilator strategy to prevent recurrence.
Reveal model answer

1. Description of the abnormality

  • Flow–time trace: expiratory flow (below baseline) fails to return to zero before the next mandatory breath is delivered — the hallmark of incomplete exhalation/gas trapping.
  • Pressure–time trace: progressive breath-to-breath rise in both baseline (end-expiratory) pressure and peak inspiratory pressure — consistent with build-up of intrinsic PEEP (auto-PEEP) and dynamic hyperinflation, i.e. breath stacking.

2. Pathophysiology and link to hypotension

  • Severe airflow obstruction (bronchospasm, airway oedema, mucus plugging) markedly prolongs the time constant for exhalation; at a fixed rate and inspiratory time, there is insufficient time for full lung emptying before the next breath is delivered.
  • Progressive air trapping raises end-expiratory lung volume and intrathoracic pressure (auto-PEEP), which impedes venous return to the right heart and compresses the pulmonary vasculature, reducing RV preload and increasing RV afterload — this produces obstructive-physiology hypotension that can mimic (or coexist with) tension pneumothorax or cardiac tamponade.
  • Reduced venous return and increased intrathoracic pressure both reduce cardiac output, which is magnified immediately post-intubation by sedative-induced venodilation and loss of sympathetic drive.

3. Immediate action

  • Disconnect the patient from the ventilator circuit and allow full passive exhalation (manually decompress the chest if needed) — this is the single most important immediate manoeuvre and should be done before troubleshooting the ventilator settings.
  • Simultaneously perform a rapid clinical assessment to exclude tension pneumothorax as an alternative or additional cause of peri-intubation hypotension (asymmetric chest movement, absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation) — the two can coexist and both cause obstructive shock.
  • If the patient improves with disconnection, this confirms dynamic hyperinflation as the (or a) cause; give IV fluid bolus to support preload while the ventilator strategy is corrected.

4. Subsequent ventilator strategy

  • Adopt a strategy that maximises expiratory time: reduce respiratory rate, reduce minute ventilation target, increase inspiratory flow rate to shorten inspiratory time (accepting a higher peak but not plateau pressure), and consider a lower tidal volume.
  • Accept permissive hypercapnia (target pH >7.15–7.20 rather than normocapnia) as a deliberate strategy to avoid dynamic hyperinflation.
  • Set extrinsic PEEP cautiously and only after auto-PEEP is controlled — in spontaneously triggering patients with severe obstruction, a modest extrinsic PEEP (roughly 70–85% of measured auto-PEEP) can reduce triggering work, but in a fully controlled, paralysed patient without spontaneous effort, extrinsic PEEP does not help emptying and should generally be kept low or zero until obstruction eases.
  • Optimise bronchodilator therapy (continuous nebulised beta-agonist, anticholinergic, IV magnesium already given, consider IV beta-agonist or ketamine infusion for bronchodilation/sedation), treat any pneumothorax if found, and monitor with serial plateau pressure and measured auto-PEEP (end-expiratory hold) rather than relying on peak pressure alone.

Reveal model answer
  • Before: P/F = 68/0.6 ≈ 113 mmHg (severe ARDS). After: P/F = 90/0.5 = 180 mmHg (moderate ARDS) — a meaningful improvement in Berlin severity category.
  • OI = (FiO2 × 100 × mean airway pressure) / PaO2 (mmHg). Before: (0.6×100×14)/68 ≈ 12.4. After: (0.5×100×12)/90 ≈ 6.7.   • Both metrics improve together after proning, supporting continued use of the intervention — both should be trended alongside clinical context rather than relying on a single number at a single time point.
  • OI is historically more used in paediatric/neonatal ARDS and in ECMO referral criteria in some settings, complementing P/F-ratio-based criteria such as those informing the EOLIA-based referral thresholds discussed in CCS_L01.

CBS_BIO_28 — Critical Illness-Related Corticosteroid Insufficiency: The Cortisol Testing Controversy

Biochemistry / Data Sepsis / Infection CoBaTrICE 2·3·4 12 marks · 12 min

Clinical context

A patient with septic shock remains on noradrenaline 0.4 micrograms/kg/min despite adequate resuscitation. The team debates whether to check a random cortisol or perform a short synacthen test before starting hydrocortisone.

Structured questions

  1. Explain the concept of critical illness-related corticosteroid insufficiency (CIRCI).
  2. Why is routine cortisol/synacthen testing before starting hydrocortisone no longer recommended?
  3. What should actually drive the decision to start hydrocortisone in this scenario?
  4. What is the practical implication for this patient right now?
Reveal model answer
  • CIRCI describes relative adrenal insufficiency/tissue glucocorticoid resistance during critical illness — historically diagnosed via the short synacthen (ACTH stimulation) test, looking for an inadequate cortisol rise.
  • Routine testing is no longer recommended by current guidelines (Surviving Sepsis Campaign, SCCM/ESICM CIRCI guidance) because total cortisol measurement is unreliable in critical illness (altered protein binding from low albumin, assay variability), free cortisol is not routinely available, and the synacthen test has poor reproducibility/predictive value for who benefits from steroids in septic shock.
  • The decision should be based on the clinical scenario — refractory shock with an ongoing significant vasopressor requirement (as discussed in CCS_S01) — not a biochemical test result.
  • Do not delay starting hydrocortisone in refractory septic shock while awaiting or performing a synacthen test; if a random cortisol is checked as part of a broader work-up, it should not gate the treatment decision.
Marking guide

12 marks: correct CIRCI concept (3), correct rationale for abandoning routine testing (4), correct clinical-scenario-based decision criterion (3), correct "do not delay treatment" practical answer (2).

Guideline-drift trap: Ordering and awaiting a short synacthen test or random cortisol level before starting hydrocortisone in refractory septic shock is outdated practice — current guidelines specifically recommend against routine adrenal function testing to guide this decision, since these tests are unreliable in critical illness.
