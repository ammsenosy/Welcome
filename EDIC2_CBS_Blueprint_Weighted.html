How to use this resource

This bank contains 200 EDIC Part II–style stations:

  • 50 CCS (20 long, 30 short)
  • 150 CBS (50 imaging, 50 curves/waveforms, 50 biochemistry/data)

All stations are consultant-level, aligned with the EDIC Guidelines 2023–2026 and the CoBaTrICE syllabus.

Suggested approach

CCS stations

  • Long cases: allow yourself 10 minutes to read the opening vignette, then 25 minutes to answer out loud in structured bullet points, as in the real exam.
  • Short cases: 5 minutes reading, 12–13 minutes answering.
  • Answer out loud first, then write your bullet points. If you cannot say it in structured order in 60 seconds, you do not truly have it.
  • Before revealing the model answer, set your confidence: Confident / Unsure / Guessing. Do not adjust it afterwards.
  • Then reveal the model answer and mark yourself as an examiner would. Partial credit is allowed; benefit of the doubt is not.

CBS stations

  • Treat each as a 12-minute station with 8–12 expected answers.
  • Go through the images/curves/data in order, speaking your answers out loud, then write them down.
  • Set confidence before revealing, then mark yourself strictly.

Tracking your progress

  • For each station, record: confidence, marks awarded, and total marks.
  • Periodically review your performance by domain (respiratory, cardiovascular, neuro, etc.) to identify weak areas.
  • Re-sit selected stations after 2–4 weeks without re-reading the model answers first; the delta is the only measure that matters.

Blueprint coverage

  • The distribution of stations follows the EDIC Part II blueprint derived from CoBaTrICE:
    • Heavy emphasis on respiratory, cardiovascular, neurology, and infection.
    • Substantial coverage of renal/metabolic, GI/hepatic, haematology/oncology, endocrine/metabolic-toxic, obstetric, trauma, and end-of-life/ethics.
  • CBS stations emphasise data interpretation (imaging, curves, biochemistry) across all systems.

Use this bank as a calibration and gap-diagnostic tool, not just a question list. The goal is to expose guideline drift, strengthen structured verbal answers, and build exam-ready fluency.

CBS_IMG_01 — Portable Chest Radiograph in Severe Hypoxaemic Respiratory Failure

Imaging Respiratory CoBaTrICE 2·3·4 10 marks · 12 min

Clinical context

A 61-year-old woman, ventilated for 36 hours for severe community-acquired pneumonia, has a supine portable AP chest radiograph performed for worsening oxygenation (PaO2/FiO2 95 mmHg). Endotracheal tube and a right internal jugular central line are in place.

Structured questions

  1. Describe this radiograph systematically, as you would present it to an examiner.
  2. What is your differential diagnosis for this pattern, and which single bedside investigation would most efficiently narrow it in this patient?
  3. Relate the appearance to a formal diagnostic framework for this patient's respiratory failure.
  4. Name three further tests or manoeuvres you would perform in the next hour and what each would add.
Reveal model answer

1. Systematic description

  • Confirm patient details, projection (AP, supine, portable), rotation and inspiratory effort — adequacy should be caveated for a portable supine film.
  • Lines/tubes: endotracheal tube tip appropriately positioned above the carina (roughly 3–5 cm above, mid-trachea with neck neutral); right internal jugular central line with tip projecting appropriately at the cavoatrial junction — state you would confirm on a magnified view.
  • Lung fields: bilateral, fairly symmetric, diffuse airspace (alveolar) opacification with visible air bronchograms, sparing no zone — consistent with diffuse alveolar filling rather than a focal process.
  • Heart size: cannot be reliably assessed on a supine AP film (cardiothoracic ratio not valid), but no gross cardiomegaly apparent; no obvious upper-lobe diversion or peribronchial cuffing to strongly favour cardiogenic oedema, though supine positioning limits this assessment.
  • Pleura: no obvious pneumothorax or large effusion; costophrenic angles not fully assessable supine.
  • Soft tissues/bones: no surgical emphysema, no obvious fracture.

2. Differential diagnosis and bedside investigation

  • Differential: ARDS (from pneumonia/sepsis as here), cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, diffuse alveolar haemorrhage, aspiration, acute interstitial pneumonia/acute eosinophilic pneumonia, less likely miliary infection given acuity.
  • The single most efficient bedside test to narrow this is focused critical care echocardiography — assessing LV systolic function, filling status, and excluding significant cardiogenic contribution, which is central to the Berlin Definition's requirement to exclude cardiac failure as the sole/main explanation.

3. Formal diagnostic framework

  • Apply the Berlin Definition of ARDS: acute onset (within 1 week of a known insult — here, pneumonia), bilateral opacities not fully explained by effusion/collapse/nodules, and respiratory failure not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload (requires objective assessment, e.g. echo, if no clear ARDS risk factor — though a clear risk factor is present here).
  • With PaO2/FiO2 95 mmHg on adequate PEEP, this represents moderate ARDS (101–200 mmHg band; note 95 mmHg would be at the moderate/severe boundary — confirm the PEEP level at which this was measured, as the ratio is only valid at PEEP/CPAP ≥5 cmH2O).

4. Further tests/manoeuvres in the next hour

  • Focused echocardiography — as above, to exclude a significant cardiogenic contribution and assess RV function/strain from high intrathoracic pressures.
  • Repeat/confirm ETT and line position on a magnified view or by direct laryngoscopy if any doubt, given the implications of a malpositioned airway device in a hypoxaemic patient.
  • Lung ultrasound at the bedside — rapid, radiation-free assessment for B-lines (interstitial/alveolar syndrome), effusion, and to help exclude pneumothorax; complements the radiograph and can be repeated serially without transport risk.
Marking guide

Award up to 10 marks across: systematic ABCDE-style description (3), correct identification of ETT/line position as first priority (1), a coherent differential diagnosis (2), correct application of the Berlin Definition with correct severity band (2), sensible next steps with justification (2).

Common error / drift trap: Candidates frequently attempt to comment on cardiothoracic ratio or use classic upright-film cardiomegaly criteria on a supine AP portable film — this is invalid and should be explicitly flagged as a limitation rather than quantified. Similarly, candidates should not state the PaO2/FiO2 ratio is diagnostic in isolation without confirming it was measured on PEEP/CPAP ≥5 cmH2O.

CBS_CRV_01 — Ventilator Waveforms in Severe Asthma: Dynamic Hyperinflation

Curves / Waveforms Respiratory CoBaTrICE 2·3·4·5 10 marks · 12 min

Clinical context

A 26-year-old man is intubated for status asthmaticus refractory to nebulised bronchodilators and IV magnesium. Ten minutes after intubation, on volume-control ventilation (tidal volume 400 mL, respiratory rate 22/min, no extrinsic PEEP set), the bedside monitor shows the flow–time and pressure–time waveforms below, and his blood pressure has fallen from 118/70 to 78/45 mmHg.

Search link only (direct/topic-page links were dropped — less reliable and not verifiable from this session). Check the site's licence before reusing or redistributing content.

Structured questions

  1. Describe the abnormality shown in each trace.
  2. What is the underlying pathophysiology, and how does it explain the fall in blood pressure?
  3. What is your immediate action given the hypotension, before any ventilator adjustment?
  4. Outline your subsequent ventilator strategy to prevent recurrence.
Reveal model answer

1. Description of the abnormality

  • Flow–time trace: expiratory flow (below baseline) fails to return to zero before the next mandatory breath is delivered — the hallmark of incomplete exhalation/gas trapping.
  • Pressure–time trace: progressive breath-to-breath rise in both baseline (end-expiratory) pressure and peak inspiratory pressure — consistent with build-up of intrinsic PEEP (auto-PEEP) and dynamic hyperinflation, i.e. breath stacking.

2. Pathophysiology and link to hypotension

  • Severe airflow obstruction (bronchospasm, airway oedema, mucus plugging) markedly prolongs the time constant for exhalation; at a fixed rate and inspiratory time, there is insufficient time for full lung emptying before the next breath is delivered.
  • Progressive air trapping raises end-expiratory lung volume and intrathoracic pressure (auto-PEEP), which impedes venous return to the right heart and compresses the pulmonary vasculature, reducing RV preload and increasing RV afterload — this produces obstructive-physiology hypotension that can mimic (or coexist with) tension pneumothorax or cardiac tamponade.
  • Reduced venous return and increased intrathoracic pressure both reduce cardiac output, which is magnified immediately post-intubation by sedative-induced venodilation and loss of sympathetic drive.

3. Immediate action

  • Disconnect the patient from the ventilator circuit and allow full passive exhalation (manually decompress the chest if needed) — this is the single most important immediate manoeuvre and should be done before troubleshooting the ventilator settings.
  • Simultaneously perform a rapid clinical assessment to exclude tension pneumothorax as an alternative or additional cause of peri-intubation hypotension (asymmetric chest movement, absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation) — the two can coexist and both cause obstructive shock.
  • If the patient improves with disconnection, this confirms dynamic hyperinflation as the (or a) cause; give IV fluid bolus to support preload while the ventilator strategy is corrected.

4. Subsequent ventilator strategy

  • Adopt a strategy that maximises expiratory time: reduce respiratory rate, reduce minute ventilation target, increase inspiratory flow rate to shorten inspiratory time (accepting a higher peak but not plateau pressure), and consider a lower tidal volume.
  • Accept permissive hypercapnia (target pH >7.15–7.20 rather than normocapnia) as a deliberate strategy to avoid dynamic hyperinflation.
  • Set extrinsic PEEP cautiously and only after auto-PEEP is controlled — in spontaneously triggering patients with severe obstruction, a modest extrinsic PEEP (roughly 70–85% of measured auto-PEEP) can reduce triggering work, but in a fully controlled, paralysed patient without spontaneous effort, extrinsic PEEP does not help emptying and should generally be kept low or zero until obstruction eases.
  • Optimise bronchodilator therapy (continuous nebulised beta-agonist, anticholinergic, IV magnesium already given, consider IV beta-agonist or ketamine infusion for bronchodilation/sedation), treat any pneumothorax if found, and monitor with serial plateau pressure and measured auto-PEEP (end-expiratory hold) rather than relying on peak pressure alone.
Marking guide

Award up to 10 marks across: correct description of both traces (2), correct pathophysiological explanation linking auto-PEEP to obstructive hypotension (2), correct immediate action — ventilator disconnection before troubleshooting settings (2), exclusion of pneumothorax as a differential/co-existing cause (1), coherent subsequent ventilator strategy including permissive hypercapnia (2), safe and appropriately cautious approach to extrinsic PEEP in this context (1).

Common error / drift trap: A frequent error is reflexively increasing extrinsic PEEP to "treat" high pressures without first recognising dynamic hyperinflation and disconnecting the circuit — this can worsen hyperinflation and hypotension. Extrinsic PEEP is a tool to offset the trigger workload of auto-PEEP in a spontaneously breathing patient, not a first-line treatment for breath stacking in a controlled, paralysed patient.

  2. Leatherman J. Mechanical ventilation for severe asthma. Chest. 2015;147(6):1671–1680.
  3. Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA). Global Strategy for Asthma Management and Prevention. Available at: ginasthma.org

CBS_BIO_01 — High Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis: Delta Ratio and Compensation

Biochemistry / Data Renal / Metabolic / Acid–base CoBaTrICE 2·3·4 12 marks · 12 min

Clinical context

A 22-year-old woman with known type 1 diabetes is brought to the emergency department drowsy, tachypnoeic, and clinically dehydrated after 3 days of vomiting and reduced insulin intake during a viral illness.

Structured questions

  1. Interpret this arterial blood gas systematically, stating the primary disturbance and whether compensation is appropriate.
  2. Calculate the anion gap and the delta ratio. What does the delta ratio tell you here?
  3. What is the diagnosis, and how would you distinguish it from hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS)?
  4. Outline your immediate management priorities for the first 60 minutes.
Reveal model answer

1. Systematic ABG interpretation

  • pH 7.12 → severe acidaemia.
  • Bicarbonate markedly low (8 mmol/L) with low PaCO2 (22 mmHg) → primary metabolic acidosis with respiratory compensation.
  • Check appropriateness of compensation using Winter's formula: expected PaCO2 (mmHg) = 1.5 × [HCO3−] + 8 ± 2 = 1.5 × 8 + 8 ± 2 = 20 ± 2 mmHg (range 18–22 mmHg). Measured PaCO2 of 22 mmHg falls within this range, i.e. respiratory compensation is appropriate — there is no independent respiratory disorder to explain further on top of the expected compensation.

2. Anion gap and delta ratio

  • Anion gap = Na+ − (Cl− + HCO3−) = 138 − (100 + 8) = 30 mmol/L (markedly elevated; normal ≈8–12 mmol/L) → high anion gap metabolic acidosis, consistent with ketoacidosis given the markedly raised ketones.
  • Delta ratio = (anion gap − 12) / (24 − HCO3−) = (30 − 12) / (24 − 8) = 18/16 ≈ 1.1.
  • A delta ratio between roughly 1 and 2 indicates a pure high anion gap metabolic acidosis with no significant concurrent normal anion gap (hyperchloraemic) process and no significant concurrent metabolic alkalosis. (A ratio <1 would suggest a co-existing normal anion gap acidosis; a ratio >2 would suggest a co-existing metabolic alkalosis or pre-existing elevated bicarbonate.)

3. Diagnosis and distinction from HHS

  • Diagnosis: diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) — hyperglycaemia, raised ketones, high anion gap metabolic acidosis with pH <7.3 and bicarbonate <15 mmol/L, in a patient with reduced insulin intake during intercurrent illness.
  • Key distinguishing features from HHS: DKA typically has significant ketosis/acidosis (as here) and glucose is often (though not always) lower than in HHS; HHS is characterised by profound hyperglycaemia (often >30 mmol/L, frequently much higher), markedly raised osmolality, minimal ketosis/acidosis (pH usually >7.3, bicarbonate usually >15 mmol/L), and a more insidious onset, typically in older patients with type 2 diabetes. This patient's significant ketosis and acidaemia clearly place her in DKA rather than HHS, even though her glucose and osmolality are also elevated — the two conditions exist on a spectrum and can overlap.
  • Calculate corrected sodium for the degree of hyperglycaemia (add ~2–2.4 mmol/L to measured sodium for every 5.5 mmol/L glucose above normal) — relevant to true free-water deficit assessment and to avoid missing a falsely low/normal sodium.

4. Immediate management (first 60 minutes)

  • IV fluid resuscitation with isotonic crystalloid (e.g. 0.9% saline), assessing haemodynamic status first — she is clinically dehydrated and drowsy, so cautious but prompt volume resuscitation is required.
  • Fixed-rate IV insulin infusion (e.g. 0.1 units/kg/hr) — do not withhold insulin despite the elevated potassium; insulin is essential to switch off ketogenesis.
  • Potassium monitoring and early replacement plan: although potassium is currently 5.6 mmol/L, total body potassium is depleted and insulin therapy will drive potassium intracellularly — plan to add potassium replacement to IV fluids once potassium falls below ~5.5 mmol/L, with frequent re-checks (hourly initially).
  • Do not give bicarbonate routinely — reserve for extreme, life-threatening acidaemia (e.g. pH <6.9) with senior/consultant input, as routine bicarbonate has not been shown to improve outcomes and may cause paradoxical CNS acidosis and worsen hypokalaemia.
  • Identify and treat the precipitant (here, likely intercurrent viral illness plus reduced insulin dosing) — septic screen if indicated, avoid missing a co-existing infective trigger.
  • Monitor for cerebral oedema risk (more relevant in younger patients/adolescents) with a cautious, gradual correction strategy; hourly neuro-observations, glucose, and venous gas/ketone monitoring per DKA protocol (e.g. JBDS-IP) until resolution criteria are met (ketones <0.6 mmol/L, venous pH >7.3, bicarbonate >18 mmol/L).
Marking guide

Award up to 12 marks: correct primary disturbance and compensation assessment with Winter's formula (2), correct anion gap calculation (1), correct delta ratio calculation and interpretation (2), correct diagnosis of DKA with justification (2), correct differentiation from HHS (2), fluids/insulin/potassium management (2), correct bicarbonate caveat (1).

Common error / drift trap: Candidates often withhold insulin because the potassium is "high" — this is incorrect; insulin is required to resolve ketoacidosis and hyperkalaemia here reflects a transcellular shift, not true total-body excess. Equally common is reflexive administration of bicarbonate for a low pH without applying the specific, narrow threshold (pH <6.9) supported by current DKA guidelines.
